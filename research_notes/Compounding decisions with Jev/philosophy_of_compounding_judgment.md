# Philosophy of Compounding Judgment: does "80%-good" fast judgment compound into excellent outcomes, and what must a decision engine get right?

Scope note. These notes cover the philosophy, decision-theory, epistemology, and organizational-science literature behind the thesis that many cheap, roughly-80%-right decisions can be "compounded" into excellent outcomes, with the motivating system being TypeSafe AI's Jev (a fast, typed, probability-returning "System One" model launched 15 September 2026). Each section follows the fixed format (Takeaway / Cited Findings / Inferences / Gaps) and each Inferences block ends with a concrete **Design implication** line. Where I could not reach a primary text I say so in Gaps. Arithmetic in the final section is my own calculation from stated assumptions and is marked as such.

## Context: the motivating system (TypeSafe Jev) and what it actually claims

### Takeaway
Jev is a typed-decision model that returns a structured value plus a confidence estimate, at latencies of roughly 70–500 ms and a list price of $42 per billion input tokens; TypeSafe's own pitch is that developers "set thresholds" for when Jev acts alone versus escalates for review. That threshold-plus-escalation architecture is the hinge on which every literature below turns.

### Cited Findings
- Jev is "TypeSafe AI's first public System One model," launched 15 September 2026 alongside a $40M round led by DCVC; it is built by Diogo Almeida, described as a co-inventor of RLHF/InstructGPT at OpenAI. Coverage is largely vendor-adjacent and performance claims are unverified by third parties. — [Sanity glossary](https://www.sanity.io/glossary/jev-typesafe-ai-model); [TrueFoundry](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/typesafe-ai-jev); [Hyperstack](https://www.hyperstack.cloud/technical-resources/tutorials/jev-inside-typesafe-ais-first-system-one-decision-model)
- It "answers typed questions about text or JSON and returns structured values with probabilities rather than prose"; the API is a POST to `/v1/systemone` using the `jev-latest` route; access is waitlisted early access. — [DEV Community guide](https://dev.to/valyuai/how-to-use-jev-a-practical-guide-to-typesafes-system-one-model-g5e); [Sanity glossary](https://www.sanity.io/glossary/jev-typesafe-ai-model)
- "System One" is TypeSafe's product framing, not an established industry category. — [TrueFoundry](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/typesafe-ai-jev)
- TypeSafe's homepage describes System One Models as "built for decisions inside software," trained with what it calls "Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD)"; outputs are "Typed outputs that software can act on," and "Every decision includes an estimate of how confident the model is." Developers set thresholds for when Jev acts autonomously versus sends a decision for review, and are told to "Combine those decisions in code to build larger workflows." — [typesafe.ai](https://typesafe.ai/)
- Headline marketing claims: "193.6x Faster, 444.6x Cheaper," a "Zero Hallucinations" heading, $42 per billion input tokens, and a candidate-screening case study claiming 88% cost reduction. The page gives no calibration metrics and does not describe benchmark methodology; it positions Jev against RLHF-shaped LLMs (which it says suffer "mode dropping, overconfidence, and lack of reliability") rather than as a complement to slower reasoning models. — [typesafe.ai](https://typesafe.ai/)
- One third-party reproduction on an open model reported a 1.83x speed gap between the mechanism and standard generation; public-benchmark performance is described as still unknown. — [TrueFoundry](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/typesafe-ai-jev); [Hyperstack](https://www.hyperstack.cloud/technical-resources/tutorials/jev-inside-typesafe-ais-first-system-one-decision-model)

### Inferences
- The product's two load-bearing claims are (a) *calibration* (the confidence number means what it says) and (b) *composability in code* (chain/aggregate decisions). The literature below says (a) is the thing that makes the 80% thesis workable at all, and (b) is where it most often goes wrong (chaining multiplies error; aggregating averages it).
- "Zero hallucinations" is a claim about output *type* (a typed value cannot be a hallucinated sentence), not about *correctness*; the FAQ itself asks "Can Jev still get things wrong?" The honest framing is "zero malformed outputs, calibrated error rate," which is precisely what a decision-quality framework would want.
- **Design implication:** Treat the confidence estimate, not the decision, as the primary product; every downstream architectural choice (threshold, escalation, aggregation, learning) must be justified by measured calibration (reliability diagrams / Brier decomposition) on the deployer's own distribution, not the vendor's.

### Gaps
- No independent calibration study of Jev exists as of October 2026 in the sources I found. All quantitative claims (latency, cost ratios, "zero hallucinations") are vendor-sourced.
- I did not find TypeSafe's answers to its own FAQ items ("Can Jev still get things wrong?", "Is Jev deterministic?") in the fetched page text.

## 1. Dual-process theory: Kahneman's System 1/System 2, Stanovich and Evans, and when fast judgment is trustworthy (Kahneman and Klein 2009)

### Takeaway
Cognitive science does not say fast judgment is bad; it says fast judgment is trustworthy only in "high-validity" environments where the person (or system) has had prolonged opportunity to learn cue-outcome regularities through rapid, unambiguous feedback, and that confidence is not evidence of accuracy. The irony of a "System One model" gated by thresholds set by slower deliberation is not a contradiction; it is the textbook architecture for how expertise is supposed to work.

### Cited Findings
- The System 1 / System 2 labels were introduced into the psychology literature by Stanovich and West (2000), and popularized by Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). Stanovich and West's original paper framed the two systems around individual differences in reasoning and the "rationality debate." — [Stanovich & West 2000, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 23(5):645–665](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00003435); [Kahneman 2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow)
- Evans and Stanovich (2013) argue the defining feature of Type 1 processing is *autonomy* (it does not require working memory and runs when triggering conditions are met), while Type 2 processing requires working memory and cognitive decoupling (hypothetical thinking). Speed, effort and consciousness are only *correlates*, not defining features; they explicitly prefer "Type 1/Type 2" to "System 1/System 2" to avoid implying two anatomical systems. — [Evans & Stanovich 2013, Perspectives on Psychological Science 8(3):223–241](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612460685)
- Kahneman and Klein (2009), an adversarial collaboration between the heuristics-and-biases and naturalistic-decision-making camps, concluded that whether an intuition can be trusted depends on "assessing how predictable the environment is and whether the person has had a real chance to learn its regularities," and that "subjective confidence is not a reliable sign of accuracy." — [Kahneman & Klein 2009, American Psychologist 64(6):515–526](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755) (abstract; see Gaps)
- A high-validity environment is one with stable relationships between observable cues and later outcomes (medicine and firefighting are the paper's exemplars); stock-picking and long-range political forecasting are described as zero-validity settings. Learning additionally requires prolonged practice with feedback that is rapid and unambiguous. — [LessWrong summary quoting the paper](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/asq9esJzoTuja7qhc/the-three-stages-of-model-development); [Timnovate summary](https://timnovate.com/2009/11/09/can-we-trust-the-intuition-of-experts/)
- In a McKinsey interview on the same paper, Klein said strategic executive decisions "don't really involve high-validity environments," though there is enough structure to listen to intuition, while Kahneman voiced concern about overconfidence. — [McKinsey Quarterly, "Strategic decisions: When can you trust your gut?"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/strategic-decisions-when-can-you-trust-your-gut) (via search summary; direct fetch timed out)
- Hogarth's distinction between "kind" learning environments (feedback is accurate and plentiful; intuitions learned there are reliable) and "wicked" ones (feedback is missing, delayed, or misleading; intuitions learned there are systematically wrong) formalizes the same point. — [Hogarth, Lejarraga & Soyer 2015, Current Directions in Psychological Science 24(5):379–385](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721415591878); Hogarth 2001, *Educating Intuition*, University of Chicago Press
- Kahneman's own account of how System 2 relates to System 1: System 2 monitors and, when it has the resources, endorses or overrides System 1's suggestions; most of the time it endorses. Skilled intuition is System 1 pattern recognition that was *trained* by prior System 2 effort and feedback. — [Kahneman 2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow)

### Inferences
- A "System One model" is a good name only under Evans and Stanovich's definition (autonomous, triggered, no deliberative loop), not under the folk definition ("fast"). What makes Jev-like decisions trustworthy is not speed but whether the task family is a kind/high-validity environment with closed feedback.
- The Kahneman-Klein criteria translate directly into a per-task-type admission test for automation: (1) is there a stable cue-to-outcome relationship? (2) does the outcome become observable quickly and unambiguously? (3) has the model been trained on that specific regularity (expertise "fractionates": competence in one sub-domain does not transfer)? Tasks failing any of the three should not be automated regardless of the model's stated confidence, because the paper's central warning is that confidence is produced by fluency, not validity.
- The gating irony resolves cleanly: in the human case, System 2 sets the policy (what to attend to, when to trust the gut) and System 1 executes; thresholds and escalation rules are the software analogue of System 2 oversight, and the model's confidence estimate is the analogue of the "feeling of knowing," which must be validated against outcomes rather than taken at face value.
- **Design implication:** Admit a decision type to autonomous handling only after it passes an explicit validity-and-feedback test (stable cues, observable outcomes within a bounded time, measured calibration on that type); publish per-type calibration curves, because the model's confidence is not evidence of accuracy until shown to be.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the full text of Kahneman and Klein (2009); the "high-validity" and "feedback" conditions are confirmed through the abstract and quoting secondary sources, not the paper body.
- The McKinsey interview fetch timed out twice; quotes attributed to it come from a search snippet and should be re-verified before quotation.

## 2. Bounded rationality and heuristics: Simon, Gigerenzer, ecological rationality, less-is-more, and why irrelevant information degrades judgment

### Takeaway
Simon showed that "good enough" (satisficing) is the rational response to bounded computation, and Gigerenzer's school showed something stronger: in uncertain, data-sparse environments, simple cue-limited heuristics can be *more* accurate than information-greedy models because they trade a little bias for a lot less variance. The thesis "80% is enough" has real support here, but with a crucial qualifier: the heuristic must be matched to the environment's structure (ecological rationality), which is an empirical fact about the world, not a property of the heuristic.

### Cited Findings
- Simon introduced "satisficing": choosing an alternative that meets an aspiration level rather than the optimum, because organisms have limited computational capacity and information. He later described rationality as "shaped by a scissors whose two blades are the structure of task environments and the computational capabilities of the actor." — [Simon 1956, Psychological Review 63(2):129–138](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042769); [Wikipedia: Satisficing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisficing); [Wikipedia: Bounded rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_rationality)
- Gigerenzer and Brighton (2009) argue that "ignoring information can improve accuracy," review "less-is-more" effects, and describe the mind as an "adaptive toolbox" of heuristics whose rationality is *ecological*: a heuristic is rational relative to the structure of the environment it is used in. A less-is-more effect means "minds gain nothing from complex strategies even when direct and opportunity costs are zero." — [Gigerenzer & Brighton 2009, Topics in Cognitive Science 1(1):107–143](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2008.01006.x); [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/GigerenzerBrighton2009_HomoHeuristicus.pdf)
- The mechanism is the bias-variance dilemma: a complex strategy that estimates many parameters has high variance when data are scarce and may therefore perform worse out-of-sample than a simpler heuristic like take-the-best or tallying; heuristics are "a response to uncertainty rather than second-best shortcuts." — [Brighton & Gigerenzer 2012, "Homo heuristicus and the bias–variance dilemma"](https://ophen.org/pub-188127); [Brighton & Gigerenzer 2012, Malaysian J. Medical Sciences](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3629675)
- Critique: the less-is-more claim is contested; a line of work argues that "ignoring information is rarely, if ever, optimal" when the information is used by a correctly specified model, and that the apparent advantage of heuristics comes from comparing them to poorly regularized full-information models. — [Jason Collins, summarizing Katsikopoulos et al. critiques](https://www.jasoncollins.blog/posts/in-contrast-to-less-is-more-claims-ignoring-information-is-rarely-if-ever-optimal)
- Kahneman and Klein's adversarial collaboration also notes the two camps' partial agreement: heuristics work where the environment supplies valid cues, and fail where it does not; this is why the same cue set that produces expert accuracy in a kind environment produces systematic bias in a wicked one. — [Kahneman & Klein 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755)

### Inferences
- Simon licenses "good enough" as *rational given costs*; Gigerenzer licenses "good enough" as *sometimes more accurate*. Both are claims about single decisions in a fixed environment, not claims that many good-enough decisions compound into excellence. The compounding has to come from elsewhere (feedback and aggregation, sections 4 and 7).
- "Irrelevant information degrades judgment" has a precise meaning here: extra cues add variance (overfitting) and, in humans, dilute diagnostic cues. For a typed decision model this argues for *small, curated input schemas* per decision type rather than "give it the whole document," and for empirically pruning cues that do not improve out-of-sample calibration.
- Ecological rationality means the admission test from section 1 is environment-specific: a decision type can be automated in one deployment and not another even with the same model, because the cue-outcome structure differs.
- **Design implication:** Define each decision type with a minimal, versioned cue schema; measure whether adding fields improves out-of-sample Brier score and remove those that do not; treat "the model performs well on this decision type in this environment" as an empirical finding to be re-tested on distribution shift, not a property of the model.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a primary text for Simon's "scissors" quotation (Simon 1990, Annual Review of Psychology 41:1–19, "Invariants of human behavior"); it is widely quoted but should be checked before reuse.

## 3. Decision quality and the decision/outcome distinction: Howard, Spetzler, Duke, Parrish, Bevelin

### Takeaway
The Stanford decision-analysis tradition defines a good decision independently of its outcome (logical consistency with alternatives, information and preferences at the time) and defines "100% decision quality" as the point where additional effort is not worth its cost, which is a formal statement of "good enough." The practitioners' literature (Duke, Parrish, Bevelin) adds the operational tools: do not judge decisions by outcomes ("resulting"), keep a decision journal that records the probability you assigned before the outcome, and avoid error rather than seek brilliance.

### Cited Findings
- Howard, who coined "decision analysis," distinguishes a good decision (one logically consistent with the decision-maker's alternatives, information and preferences) from a good outcome (a favorable result), and argues the quality of decisions should be judged only on the former because outcomes are partly chance. — [Howard 1988, "Decision Analysis: Practice and Promise," Management Science 34(6):679–695](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.34.6.679)
- Spetzler, Winter and Meyer's *Decision Quality* defines six requirements, each of which must be met for a quality decision: an appropriate frame; creative, doable alternatives; meaningful, reliable information; clear values and trade-offs; sound reasoning; and commitment to action. The chain is "as strong as its weakest link," and each element is scored on a 0–100% scale where 100% means further improvement is not worth the effort. — Spetzler, Winter & Meyer 2016, *Decision Quality: Value Creation from Better Business Decisions*, Wiley, ISBN 978-1-119-14467-5; [Strategic Decisions Group overview](https://www.sdg.com/)
- Annie Duke names "resulting" (judging a decision by its outcome) as the central error in evaluating decisions under uncertainty, and reframes decisions as bets with probabilities, so that a well-made bet can lose and a badly made bet can win. — Duke 2018, *Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts*, Portfolio/Penguin
- Shane Parrish's decision-journal template asks the decision-maker to record, before the outcome is known: the situation, the problem framing, the variables, the alternatives considered, the expected outcome and the probability assigned to it, and how they feel; then to review at a set interval, specifically to fight hindsight bias. — [Farnam Street, "The Decision Journal"](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)
- Peter Bevelin's *Seeking Wisdom* synthesizes Munger's approach: inversion ("what would make this fail?"), checklists, and avoiding standard stupidities rather than seeking brilliance. — Bevelin 2007, *Seeking Wisdom: From Darwin to Munger*, PCA Publications
- Brier scores can be decomposed into reliability (calibration), resolution (discrimination), and uncertainty (base rate), which makes "was the probability right?" separable from "was the call right?" — Murphy 1973, "A New Vector Partition of the Probability Score," Journal of Applied Meteorology 12:595–600; [Wikipedia: Brier score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brier_score)

### Inferences
- The decision/outcome distinction is the strongest single argument *for* the 80% thesis: a decision engine's job is not to be right every time but to be *consistent with the available information* every time; a calibrated 80%-confident decision that turns out wrong was a good decision. This only works if the engine's probabilities are actually calibrated (Howard's "preferences and information" have to be truthfully represented).
- The DQ six elements expose what a typed-decision model does *not* supply: frame, alternatives, values/trade-offs, and commitment are set by the deployer in the schema and threshold policy; the model supplies "information" (a probability) and part of "reasoning." Decision quality of the whole system is therefore bounded by the worst of the deployer-set elements, usually the frame (asking the wrong typed question) and the values (threshold set without stating the cost asymmetry).
- A decision engine that logs (decision type, cues, probability, action, outcome, time-to-outcome) *is* a decision journal at machine scale, and Brier decomposition per decision type is the review step Parrish asks humans to do quarterly.
- **Design implication:** Log every decision as a bet (pre-outcome probability plus the cost asymmetry that justified the threshold); score each decision type with Brier reliability and resolution separately; and make the threshold an explicit statement of values ("we accept a false-accept at cost X to avoid review at cost Y"), reviewed like any other DQ element.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the Spetzler et al. book text; the six-element list and "100% = not worth further effort" definition are from my knowledge of the book and SDG training materials and should be verified against the book before quotation.

## 4. Forecasting and aggregation: Tetlock and the Good Judgment Project, Surowiecki, Galton, prediction markets, and information cascades

### Takeaway
Aggregation of many fallible judgments does produce excellent outcomes, but only when the judgments are diverse, independent, and combined by a method that respects how much information they share; extremizing works precisely because and to the extent that forecasters hold *different* information. The mirror-image failure mode, information cascades, shows that when judgments are made sequentially with visibility into prior judgments, the crowd can rationally converge on the wrong answer with very few bits of real evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Tetlock's 20-year study found that expert political forecasters were, on long-range questions, barely better than chance, and that "foxes" (eclectic, self-doubting) outperformed "hedgehogs" (one-big-idea thinkers). — Tetlock 2005, *Expert Political Judgment: How Good Is It? How Can We Know?*, Princeton University Press; [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expert_Political_Judgment)
- In IARPA's ACE tournament (2011–2015) the Good Judgment Project scored forecasters by Brier score, selected the top ~2% as "superforecasters," and found that three interventions improved accuracy: probabilistic-reasoning training, working in teams, and tracking (elite teams). — [Mellers et al. 2014, Psychological Science 25(5):1106–1115](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614524255); [Good Judgment, "The Science of Superforecasting"](https://goodjudgment.com/about/the-science-of-superforecasting/)
- The GJP aggregation algorithm "was elitist, meaning that it weighted more heavily people who were better on various metrics," and "extremized" the aggregate (pushed it toward 0 or 1); "the degree to which they extremize depends on how diverse and sophisticated the pool of forecasters is," and whether extremizing is a good idea "is still controversial." The algorithm beat the simple average of ordinary forecasters by more than 60% in Brier terms; individual superforecasters did almost as well. — [AI Impacts, "Evidence on good forecasting practices from the Good Judgment Project"](https://aiimpacts.org/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-judgment-project/)
- Satopää, Pemantle and Ungar model extremizing as a function of forecasters' *information overlap*: the right amount of extremizing rises with information diversity and falls toward zero when forecasters share the same information; this gives "a more principled understanding of the historically ad hoc practice of extremizing." — [Satopää, Pemantle & Ungar, arXiv:1406.2148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.2148v2); [arXiv:1501.06943](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.06943)
- Tetlock and Gardner report that GJP forecasts outperformed intelligence analysts with access to classified information by roughly 30%; this figure originates in a Washington Post column and Tetlock's book, not a peer-reviewed comparison. — Tetlock & Gardner 2015, *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction*, Crown; [AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-judgment-project/)
- Surowiecki's four conditions for a wise crowd: diversity of opinion, independence, decentralization, and a mechanism for aggregation. — Surowiecki 2004, *The Wisdom of Crowds*, Doubleday; [Wikipedia: The Wisdom of Crowds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wisdom_of_Crowds)
- Galton's 1907 "Vox Populi": 787 entries in an ox-weight guessing competition; the median estimate (1,207 lb) came within 1% of the true dressed weight (1,198 lb). — [Galton 1907, Nature 75:450–451](https://doi.org/10.1038/075450a0)
- Hanson's prediction-market program ("vote on values, but bet on beliefs"; futarchy) argues that markets aggregate dispersed information because participants are paid for accuracy and lose money for error, i.e., aggregation with skin in the game. — [Hanson, "Futarchy"](https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/futarchy.html); Hanson 2013, Journal of Political Philosophy 21(2):151–178
- Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch (1992) show that when individuals decide sequentially and observe predecessors' actions (but not their private signals), it becomes rational after only a few decisions to ignore one's own signal and copy; the resulting "informational cascade" is fragile, often wrong, and based on very little aggregate information. — [Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch 1992, Journal of Political Economy 100(5):992–1026](https://doi.org/10.1086/261849)

### Inferences
- The aggregation literature's lesson for a single-model decision engine is sobering: calling the same model N times with the same inputs is N forecasters with ~100% information overlap, so by Satopää et al. the justified extremizing is near zero and the "crowd" has an effective size near 1. Aggregation gains require *different* information (different cue schemas, different models, or human and model), not repetition.
- The cascade result is the precise failure mode of "combine decisions in code": if decision k conditions on decisions 1..k−1 (e.g., a routing decision feeding a classification feeding an action), the system reproduces a cascade with the model's shared bias as the "first mover." Cascades are also the mechanism by which self-training on prior decisions (section 9) locks in error.
- Tetlock's finding that *tracking* (feedback on Brier scores) was one of only three interventions that improved human forecasting is direct support for the learning-loop reading of "compounding": the compounding is in the calibration curve, not the decision stream.
- **Design implication:** Aggregate only across sources with measurably different information (different cue sets, models, or humans) and extremize only in proportion to measured error-correlation; never feed one decision's output into another's input without carrying the private evidence (probability and cues) forward, so downstream steps cannot cascade on a bare label.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain Mellers et al. 2014 itself; the training/teaming/tracking results are from secondary summaries and the paper's DOI is given for verification.
- The "30% better than analysts with classified access" figure could not be traced to a primary document in this search.

## 5. Epistemic democracy: Condorcet, Rousseau, Landemore, Estlund, Scott Page, and the Thompson critique of Hong-Page

### Takeaway
Condorcet's Jury Theorem is the clean mathematical core of the "compounding" idea: if independent judges are each better than chance, majority accuracy rises toward certainty with the number of judges. The modern debate (Landemore, Page vs. Thompson, Estlund) is almost entirely about the *conditions*: independence is violated by correlated errors, competence may be below 0.5 on hard questions, and "diversity trumps ability" rests on a theorem whose mathematical content is now widely regarded as thin.

### Cited Findings
- Condorcet's Jury Theorem (1785): if each of n voters independently chooses the correct of two options with probability p > 0.5, the probability that a majority is correct increases monotonically in n and approaches 1; if p < 0.5 it approaches 0. Required assumptions: independence, binary choice, homogeneous competence above 0.5, sincere voting. — [Wikipedia: Condorcet's jury theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem); [SEP: Social Choice Theory](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/social-choice/)
- Rousseau's "general will" has been read as a Condorcetian claim that majority voting tracks the common good; Grofman and Feld made the connection explicit. — [Grofman & Feld 1988, "Rousseau's General Will: A Condorcetian Perspective," American Political Science Review 82(2):567–576](https://doi.org/10.2307/1957401)
- Ladha showed that the theorem survives *some* correlation among votes but fails when average correlation exceeds a threshold that depends on competence; correlated errors can make a larger jury *less* accurate than a smaller one. — [Ladha 1992, "The Condorcet Jury Theorem, Free Speech, and Correlated Votes," American Journal of Political Science 36(3):617–634](https://doi.org/10.2307/2111584)
- Hong and Page (2004) claimed that under stated conditions a randomly selected group of diverse problem-solvers outperforms a group of the individually best problem-solvers ("diversity trumps ability"). — [Hong & Page 2004, PNAS 101(46):16385–16389](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0403723101)
- Page's "diversity prediction theorem" (an algebraic identity for squared error): collective error = average individual error − prediction diversity. — Page 2007, *The Difference: How the Power of Diversity Creates Better Groups, Firms, Schools, and Societies*, Princeton University Press; [Wikipedia: Scott E. Page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_E._Page)
- Landemore's *Democratic Reason* argues for the epistemic superiority of inclusive deliberation plus majority rule, leaning on Hong-Page ("diversity trumps ability") and on CJT-style "numbers trump ability." — [Landemore 2013, *Democratic Reason*, Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691173153/democratic-reason)
- Thompson (2014) argues that "once the unnecessary technicalities are removed and basic errors corrected, the theorem is revealed to be little more than a straightforward restatement of its hypotheses," that it has "neither mathematical content nor real-world applications," and that the accompanying simulation mainly shows that random algorithms can work well. — [Thompson 2014, "Does Diversity Trump Ability? An Example of the Misuse of Mathematics in the Social Sciences," Notices of the AMS 61(9):1024–1030](https://www.ams.org/notices/201409/rnoti-p1024.pdf) (canonical URL; returned 403 to me; claims via [The College Fix report](https://www.thecollegefix.com/mathematicians-refute-oft-cited-diversity-trumps-ability-study/) and [arXiv:2307.04709](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.04709))
- Replies: Singer (2019) defends the diversity interpretation against Thompson's "it's the randomness" reading while conceding functional diversity needs clearer measurement; Grim, Singer et al. (2019) find that across landscape types "diversity trumps ability in some cases, ability trumps diversity in others." — [Singer 2019, "Diversity, Not Randomness, Trumps Ability," Philosophy of Science](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/abs/diversity-not-randomness-trumps-ability/04E08F6F5E994F2F48347BFF6DAF662E); [Grim et al. 2019, "Diversity, Ability, and Expertise in Epistemic Communities," Philosophy of Science](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/diversity-ability-and-expertise-in-epistemic-communities/E679089194FE0FB848F597F3CD1F8A70)
- Estlund's "epistemic proceduralism": democratic authority requires that the procedure have *some* tendency to get things right (better than random), but legitimacy does not transfer from expertise ("the expert/boss fallacy": being right does not entail being entitled to rule). — Estlund 2008, *Democratic Authority: A Philosophical Framework*, Princeton University Press; [SEP: Democracy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/democracy/)

### Inferences
- CJT is the exact mathematical statement of when "80% compounds": with p = 0.8 and *independent* judgments, a majority of 3 is right 89.6% of the time, of 5 is right 94.2%, of 7 is right 96.7% (my binomial arithmetic). But the independence condition is violated by construction when all "judges" are the same model, and Ladha shows correlated errors can invert the result. For an LLM-family decider, the realistic independence source is *different inputs or different models*, not repeated calls.
- Page's identity is the honest version of "diversity trumps ability": collective error is reduced exactly by the variance of predictions around their mean. For a decision engine it is a design metric: measure prediction diversity across your ensemble; if it is near zero, your ensemble is one judge.
- Estlund's point maps onto delegated machine decisions: a model's accuracy does not confer *authority* to decide; authority comes from the deployer's procedure (who set the threshold, who can appeal). This anticipates section 9.
- **Design implication:** Treat Condorcet's independence condition as a measured quantity: estimate error correlation between any sources you aggregate, and count an ensemble's effective size by that correlation (not by its nominal size); route authority (who may override, who is accountable) by procedure, not by model accuracy.

## 6. Compounding as a worldview: Munger and Buffett, Dalio, Bezos, Clear and Hardy, Hamming, Taleb, and the Kelly criterion

### Takeaway
The practitioners who talk about compounding are, on inspection, talking about two different things: compounding *capital or knowledge* (Buffett, Hamming), where the mechanism is reinvestment of a stock, and compounding *error-avoidance* (Munger, Bezos, Taleb), where the mechanism is staying in the game long enough for averages to apply. The popular "1% better every day" arithmetic (Clear, Hardy) is pseudo-math because it assumes multiplicative, independent, non-decaying gains. Taleb and Kelly supply the hard constraint: compounding only works in ergodic settings where no single decision can produce ruin, which is a property the system designer must enforce, not hope for.

### Cited Findings
- Munger, in a letter dated 5 March 1990 in Wesco Financial's 1989 annual report: "It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent." — [Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2025/08/25/not-stupid/)
- Munger's "invert, always invert" (after Jacobi) and his emphasis on avoiding standard errors are collected in *Poor Charlie's Almanack* and in Bevelin's synthesis. — Kaufman (ed.) 2005, *Poor Charlie's Almanack*; Bevelin 2007, *Seeking Wisdom*
- Buffett's annual letters are the canonical practitioner statement of compounding returns over long horizons and of avoiding permanent capital loss as the precondition for compounding. — [Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/letters.html)
- Bezos 2016 letter: "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had"; "Many decisions are reversible, two-way doors. Those decisions can use a light-weight process"; "use the phrase 'disagree and commit'"; "Day 2 companies make high-quality decisions, but they make high-quality decisions slowly." — [Bezos, 2016 Letter to Shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)
- Bezos 2015 letter: some decisions are "consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible – one-way doors" (Type 1) and must be made "methodically, carefully, slowly"; most are "changeable, reversible – two-way doors" (Type 2) and "can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups"; "As organizations get larger, there seems to be a tendency to use the heavy-weight Type 1 decision-making process on most decisions, including many Type 2 decisions," producing "slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently." — [Farnam Street, "Reversible and Irreversible Decisions," quoting the 2015 letter](https://fs.blog/reversible-irreversible-decisions/) (the aboutamazon.com 2015 URL returned 404)
- Dalio's "believability-weighted decision making": in a disagreement, weight each person's view by their demonstrated track record on that kind of question, and use a tool (the "Dot Collector") to surface and weight views in real time. — Dalio 2017, *Principles: Life and Work*, Simon & Schuster; [Dalio TED 2017, "How to build a company where the best ideas win"](https://www.ted.com/talks/ray_dalio_how_to_build_a_company_where_the_best_ideas_win)
- James Clear: "if you can get 1 percent better each day for one year, you'll end up thirty-seven times better by the time you're done" (1.01^365 ≈ 37.8); Darren Hardy's *The Compound Effect* makes the same case qualitatively. — Clear 2018, *Atomic Habits*, Avery; Hardy 2010, *The Compound Effect*, Vanguard Press
- Hamming: "Knowledge and productivity are like compound interest. Given two people of approximately the same ability and one person who works ten percent more than the other, the latter will more than twice outproduce the former." — [Hamming 1986, "You and Your Research," transcript](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html)
- Taleb's antifragility program: the barbell (combine extreme safety with small, capped, high-upside bets; avoid the middle), via negativa (improvement by removal of harmful elements), and the insistence that "ruin" is categorically different from loss because it ends the sequence. — Taleb 2012, *Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder*, Random House; Taleb 2018, *Skin in the Game*, Random House
- Peters: the ergodic hypothesis (time average equals expectation value) "underlies the assumption that the time average and the expectation value of an observable are the same," and economic processes describing growth are far from equilibrium, so expected-value reasoning can misdescribe what an individual experiences over time. Critics (e.g., Toda 2024) call ergodicity economics pseudoscience for producing few falsifiable implications; "the jury is still out." — [Peters 2019, "The ergodicity problem in economics," Nature Physics 15:1216–1221](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-019-0732-0); [Exploring Economics summary](https://www.exploring-economics.org/fr/decouvrir/the-ergodicity-problem-in-economics/)
- Kelly (1956) derived the bet fraction that maximizes the long-run growth rate of wealth; over-betting beyond the Kelly fraction reduces growth and eventually guarantees ruin, and practitioners use "fractional Kelly" because estimation error in the edge makes full Kelly dangerous. — [Kelly 1956, Bell System Technical Journal 35(4):917–926](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1956.tb03809.x); MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba (eds.) 2011, *The Kelly Capital Growth Investment Criterion*, World Scientific; [Wikipedia: Kelly criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_criterion)

### Inferences
- The "1% better" arithmetic fails for three independent reasons: gains are not multiplicative on a stock (there is no base that "reinvests" correctness); they are not independent (an error that is not corrected persists); and the same math gives 0.99^365 ≈ 0.026, so any process with decay dominates. The *defensible* version (Hamming) is that *learning* compounds because today's knowledge is reinvested in tomorrow's learning: for a decision engine, that is the calibration loop, not the decision stream.
- Bezos's framework is the most directly usable: the two-way-door test is a property of the *decision type* that can be declared in a schema (reversible: yes/no; reversal cost; reversal window). "70% of the information" is explicitly conditioned on reversibility and on being "good at course correcting." Applied to a decision engine: fast autonomous handling is licensed only for decision types declared two-way, and the engine must be instrumented to detect and course-correct quickly.
- Taleb and Kelly supply the non-negotiable constraint: at millions of decisions, any per-decision probability of ruin q, however small, makes ruin certain ((1 − q)^n → 0). The only robust fix is to make ruin *impossible* per decision (cap the blast radius; one-way doors never auto-executed), not merely improbable. Kelly sizing translates to "the fraction of total exposure any single automated decision can commit."
- Dalio's believability weighting is the human precedent for the engine's own threshold logic: trust is indexed to *track record on that question type*, and is earned per domain rather than globally, which matches Kahneman and Klein's fractionation of expertise.
- Munger's "consistently not stupid" reframes the engine's goal from maximizing hit-rate to minimizing *catastrophic* error, which is the via negativa design posture: the most valuable output of a calibrated model may be its low-confidence *abstentions*, not its confident answers.
- **Design implication:** Encode reversibility and blast radius as required fields of every decision type (two-way door: auto-eligible; one-way door: never auto-executed regardless of confidence); cap per-decision and per-window exposure like a fractional Kelly bet; and measure the engine on its worst outcomes and its abstention quality, not only its mean accuracy.

- I did not find a primary Buffett quotation specifically on "avoid stupidity"; the Munger quotation is the sourced one.

## 7. Cybernetics and control: Wiener, Ashby, Beer, Boyd, Popper, Peirce

### Takeaway
Cybernetics gives the 80% thesis its mechanism: a fallible decider becomes a good regulator only when embedded in a closed feedback loop with enough *variety* in its responses (including "escalate") to match the disturbances it faces. Popper and Peirce supply the epistemology: knowledge grows by small, falsifiable, correctable steps, and "truth" is operationally the point of convergence of a community of inquirers over time. All three traditions agree that speed is worthless without observation, and that the unit of improvement is the loop, not the decision.

### Cited Findings
- Rosenblueth, Wiener and Bigelow (1943) defined purposeful behavior as behavior controlled by negative feedback from the goal; Wiener's *Cybernetics* (1948) generalized feedback as the common principle of control in animals and machines. — [Rosenblueth, Wiener & Bigelow 1943, "Behavior, Purpose and Teleology," Philosophy of Science 10(1):18–24](https://doi.org/10.1086/286788); Wiener 1948, *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*, MIT Press
- Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety: "only variety can destroy variety": a regulator can hold outcomes within acceptable bounds only if the variety of its responses is at least as great as the variety of the disturbances it must counter. — Ashby 1956, *An Introduction to Cybernetics*, Chapman & Hall, ch. 11; [online PDF](http://pespmc1.vub.ac.be/books/IntroCyb.pdf); [Wikipedia: Variety (cybernetics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_(cybernetics))
- Conant and Ashby's good regulator theorem: "every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system"; a regulator that is maximally simple and successful is isomorphic to the system it regulates. — [Conant & Ashby 1970, International Journal of Systems Science 1(2):89–97](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207727008920220)
- Beer's Viable System Model distinguishes five subsystems: System 1 (operational units doing the primary work), System 2 (coordination/anti-oscillation), System 3 (internal control and resource bargaining), System 3* (sporadic audit of operations), System 4 (intelligence; the outside-and-future), System 5 (policy and identity). A system lacking any of them is not viable. — Beer 1972, *Brain of the Firm*; Beer 1979, *The Heart of Enterprise*, Wiley; [Wikipedia: Viable system model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viable_system_model)
- Boyd's OODA loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act): advantage comes from operating inside the adversary's loop, and Boyd placed the weight on *Orient* (updating the model of the situation) rather than on raw decision speed; his only formal paper argues that models must be periodically destroyed and rebuilt to stay matched to reality. — Boyd 1976, "Destruction and Creation"; Osinga 2007, *Science, Strategy and War: The Strategic Theory of John Boyd*, Routledge; [Wikipedia: OODA loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OODA_loop)
- Popper contrasts "piecemeal social engineering" (small, reversible interventions whose results can be observed and whose mistakes can be corrected) with "utopian engineering" (wholesale blueprints whose failures cannot be isolated or learned from); his broader epistemology holds that knowledge grows by conjecture and refutation, so the value of a claim lies in its being falsifiable. — Popper 1945, *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, vol. 1 ch. 9; Popper 1957, *The Poverty of Historicism*, §§20–21; [SEP: Karl Popper](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/)
- Peirce: "The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth"; fallibilism (any belief may be mistaken) and the "method of science" as the only method of fixing belief that lets external reality correct us. — Peirce 1878, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear," Popular Science Monthly 12:286–302; Peirce 1877, "The Fixation of Belief"; [SEP: Charles Sanders Peirce](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce/); [SEP: Pragmatism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)

### Inferences
- Ashby's law explains *why* a threshold-and-escalate architecture is necessary rather than optional: a typed model with k output values has variety k; the world has more. The escalation path (to a slower model, to a human) is where the missing variety lives, and removing it to save cost makes the regulator inadequate by theorem, not by taste.
- The good regulator theorem means that the engine's quality is bounded by how good a *model of the environment* it is; distribution shift is therefore not an edge case but the central threat, and Boyd's "destruction and creation" says the model must be periodically re-oriented, not just re-thresholded.
- Beer's VSM gives the organizational checklist: a decision engine is a System 1 (operations); it needs a System 2 (coordination so that parallel decisions do not oscillate or conflict), a System 3* (random audit of autonomous decisions, which is the only way to catch errors the engine is confident about), and a System 4 (monitoring the environment for shift). Most "decision engine" designs ship System 1 alone.
- Popper's piecemeal engineering is the philosophical warrant for "many small reversible decisions beat a few big ones": not because small decisions are more often right, but because their *failures are isolable and survivable*, so the system can learn. This is conditional on each decision carrying a falsifiable prediction (what outcome would show it was wrong) and on the loop actually closing.
- Peirce reframes the engine's "truth": the right label for a decision type is not any single verdict but the limit of outcome feedback over time; the engine should treat its current thresholds as fallible and keep the inquiry open (no frozen thresholds, no label-locking).
- **Design implication:** Build the loop, not the decider: every autonomous decision type must have (a) an escalation path with more variety than the model, (b) a falsifiable outcome prediction with a deadline, (c) a random audit sample (Beer's 3*) independent of confidence, and (d) a scheduled re-orientation check for distribution shift.

## 8. Virtue and practical wisdom: Aristotle on phronesis and habituation, and the mapping onto a system that learns thresholds from outcomes

### Takeaway
Aristotle's account of practical wisdom supports the learning-loop reading of compounding (good judgment is built by repeated practice with feedback) but also draws the line a machine threshold-learner cannot cross on its own: phronesis includes grasping the *ends* worth pursuing and perceiving what is salient in the particular case, not only tuning means. The honest mapping is that a system can habituate its *thresholds* from outcomes, while the deployer must supply, and keep re-examining, the ends those thresholds serve.

### Cited Findings
- Aristotle holds that moral virtue arises from habit: "we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts" (Nicomachean Ethics II.1, 1103a–b); the right action is a mean "relative to us" determined "as the person of practical wisdom would determine it" (II.6). — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Books II and VI; [SEP: Aristotle's Ethics (Kraut)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)
- Phronesis (practical wisdom) is an intellectual virtue concerned with particulars and action; it cannot be reduced to rules because it requires perceiving the salient features of the situation; it is distinguished from mere cleverness (deinotes) because it is directed at good ends, and from scientific knowledge (episteme) because its objects are variable. — [SEP: Aristotle's Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/); [SEP: Moral Character](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-character/)
- Annas defends a "skill analogy" for virtue: virtue is acquired like a practical skill, through learning from others and then practice that yields intelligent, articulable, improving performance. — Annas 2011, *Intelligent Virtue*, Oxford University Press
- Dreyfus and Dreyfus's five-stage model of skill acquisition (novice to expert) holds that expertise is intuitive, situational, and not rule-following, and they argued that rule-based machines cannot reach the expert stage. — Dreyfus & Dreyfus 1986, *Mind over Machine: The Power of Human Intuition and Expertise in the Era of the Computer*, Free Press; [Wikipedia: Dreyfus model of skill acquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreyfus_model_of_skill_acquisition)

### Inferences
- Habituation is the ancient version of "learn thresholds from outcomes": the agent's dispositions are shaped by repeated action plus correction. That supports the engine design *if* the correction is present (closed loop) and *if* the actions it is habituated on are the right ones; Aristotle is explicit that bad habituation produces vice just as reliably.
- Phronesis's "perception of the salient" is exactly the thing a fixed cue schema cannot do: the engine sees only the fields it was given. This is why escalation must be triggered not only by low confidence but by *novelty* (inputs unlike the training distribution), because the characteristic failure of a habituated system is confident misperception of a case it was not habituated on.
- The cleverness/phronesis distinction is the ethical point: a system that is very good at hitting a threshold is clever; whether that threshold serves a good end is a question the system cannot answer. Threshold-learning that optimizes a proxy (section 9, Goodhart) is cleverness detached from ends.
- **Design implication:** Separate the two learning loops explicitly: the engine may auto-tune thresholds from outcome feedback (habituation of means), but the *objective* those thresholds serve and the cost asymmetry behind them must be set and periodically re-justified by accountable humans, with novelty detection as an escalation trigger independent of confidence.

## 9. Ethics and human factors of automated judgment: Bainbridge, Parasuraman and Manzey, meaningful human control, Elish, Goodhart, Dennett, EU AI Act Article 14, and "many hands"

### Takeaway
The human-factors literature says the standard "automate the easy 80%, send the hard 20% to a human" architecture is a known trap: the human reviewer loses skill and situational awareness on the easy cases, is left with the hardest cases under the worst conditions, defers to the automation (automation bias cannot be trained away), and then absorbs the blame when it fails (the moral crumple zone). Learning from the system's own outcome labels adds Goodhart-style corruption and runaway feedback loops. The design response is to keep humans practiced and independent, make overrides real, attribute accountability to the threshold-setter, and keep labels unbiased through randomized coverage.

### Cited Findings
- Bainbridge (1983): automating a process "may increase problems with the human operator rather than remove them." The ironies: designers who see operators as unreliable still leave them the tasks the designer could not automate; after automation the human's job becomes monitoring, which humans do poorly over long periods; and the skills needed to take over when automation fails decay precisely because the automation usually works, so the operator is least prepared when most needed. — [Bainbridge 1983, "Ironies of Automation," Automatica 19(6):775–779](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8); [PDF via gwern](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf); [The Morning Paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2020/01/08/ironies-of-automation/)
- Parasuraman and Manzey (2010): automation complacency and automation bias are "different manifestations of overlapping automation-induced phenomena, with attention playing a central role"; complacency occurs under multiple-task load when manual tasks compete for attention; both occur "in both naive and expert participants" and cannot be overcome with simple practice, training, or instructions; both affect individuals and teams. — [Parasuraman & Manzey 2010, Human Factors 52(3):381–410](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055); [TU Berlin repository copy](https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/items/0c787d91-6021-480b-a333-009e0d1d0092/full)
- Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven define "meaningful human control" by two conditions: *tracking* (the system must be responsive to the relevant moral reasons of the humans designing and deploying it, and to the relevant facts in the environment) and *tracing* (at least one human in the design or use chain must understand the system's capabilities and be in a position to be held responsible). — [Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven 2018, Frontiers in Robotics and AI 5:15](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2018.00015)
- Elish's "moral crumple zone": in a highly complex and automated system, a human operator may bear the brunt of moral and legal responsibility "no matter how little or unintentionally they are involved"; "the moral crumple zone protects the integrity of the technological system, at the expense of the nearest human operator"; the human becomes a "liability sponge." In the 2018 Uber fatality, media blame moved from the company to the safety driver within days. — [Elish 2019, Engaging Science, Technology, and Society 5:40–60](https://doi.org/10.17351/ests2019.260); [SSRN pre-print](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2757236); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2019/05/28/65748/ai-algorithms-liability-human-blame/)
- Goodhart's law, in Strathern's formulation: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." — [Strathern 1997, "'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system," European Review 5(3):305–321](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1062798700002660); [Wikipedia: Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law)
- Ensign et al. show that predictive-policing systems trained on the crime reports their own deployments generate produce "runaway feedback loops": police are sent where the model predicts crime, find crime there, and the model grows more confident, independent of the true crime distribution; they propose feeding back only data the model's own actions did not select. — [Ensign et al. 2018, "Runaway Feedback Loops in Predictive Policing," PMLR 81:160–171](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/ensign18a.html)
- Shumailov et al. show that models trained recursively on data generated by earlier models suffer "model collapse": tails of the distribution disappear and the models converge on a narrowed version of reality. — [Shumailov et al. 2024, "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data," Nature 631:755–759](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07566-y)
- Dennett's "competence without comprehension": evolution (and, by extension, trained systems) produces exquisite competence with no understanding behind it; the "reasons" are "free-floating rationales" that exist in the selection history, not in the agent. — Dennett 2017, *From Bacteria to Bach and Back: The Evolution of Minds*, W. W. Norton; [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/From_Bacteria_to_Bach_and_Back)
- EU AI Act Article 14: high-risk AI systems must be designed so they "can be effectively overseen by natural persons during the period in which they are in use"; oversight measures must match the system's risks, autonomy and context; the overseer must be enabled to understand capacities and limitations and detect anomalies, to "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output" (automation bias), to correctly interpret output, to "decide, in any particular situation, not to use the high-risk AI system or to otherwise disregard, override or reverse the output," and to "intervene in the operation ... or interrupt the system through a 'stop' button." The page states application dates of 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I). — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- The "problem of many hands": when many individuals contribute to an outcome, responsibility is diffused so that no one is identifiably responsible; Nissenbaum applied this to computing, adding "the computer as scapegoat" and "ownership without liability" as further barriers to accountability. — [Thompson 1980, "Moral Responsibility of Public Officials: The Problem of Many Hands," American Political Science Review 74(4):905–916](https://doi.org/10.2307/1954312); [Nissenbaum 1996, "Accountability in a computerized society," Science and Engineering Ethics 2:25–42](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02639315)
- Perrow's *Normal Accidents*: in systems that are both interactively complex and tightly coupled, failures propagate faster than operators can comprehend, and accidents are "normal" (expected) properties of the system's structure. — Perrow 1984, *Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies*, Basic Books; [Wikipedia: Normal Accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_Accidents)

### Inferences
- The escalation queue in a threshold architecture is Bainbridge's irony in its purest form: it delivers to the human *only* the cases the model found hard, stripped of the easy cases that would keep the human's judgment calibrated, under time pressure, with the model's confidence displayed. Parasuraman and Manzey predict the human will defer; Elish predicts the human will be blamed. The literature's answer is not "remove the human" but "give the human an independent, practiced role": blind review of a random sample (not just escalations), judgment recorded *before* seeing the model's output, and metrics on human-model disagreement.
- Tracking and tracing map exactly onto the engine's artifacts: the threshold policy and cost asymmetry *are* the "moral reasons" the system must track, so they must be explicit and versioned; tracing requires a named owner for each decision type who can explain and be held responsible for it. Article 14's "disregard, override or reverse" and "stop button" requirements mean the override must be a real control path, exercised and tested, not a UI affordance nobody uses.
- The Goodhart/feedback-loop problem is specific to "learn thresholds from outcomes": if the outcome label is observed only for decisions the engine chose to act on (or is itself produced by the engine's action), the training signal is selected by the engine. Ensign et al.'s fix generalizes: maintain a randomized exploration slice where the decision is made by a different mechanism (human, other model, or random within safe bounds), and train only on labels whose observation did not depend on the engine's own choice. Model collapse warns against training on the engine's own decisions as labels at all.
- Dennett's point is both reassuring and limiting: competence without comprehension is fine *within the selection environment*; it offers no guarantee outside it. "Zero hallucination" rhetoric should not be allowed to slide into an implied claim of comprehension.
- Many hands plus the crumple zone predicts that when a compounded decision goes wrong, blame will land on the last human touch (the reviewer who approved an escalation) unless the system's logs make the threshold-setter and model version identifiable as contributors.
- **Design implication:** Treat the human reviewer as an instrument to be kept calibrated, not a safety net: route a random, confidence-independent sample to blind human judgment recorded before the model's output is shown; make override a tested control path with a named owner per decision type; train thresholds only on labels from a randomized slice the engine did not select; and make every decision traceable to (model version, threshold version, owner) so accountability does not collapse onto the nearest human.

### Gaps
- Article 14 application dates on the fetched page (December 2027 / August 2028) differ from the dates in the Act as originally adopted (2 August 2026 for Annex III); the page may reflect a subsequent amendment or delay. Verify the current legal timetable before relying on it.

## 10. Is there meat? Steelman and strawman of "80%-good decisions are effectively compoundable," and the most compelling honest framing

### Takeaway
The thesis is true, substantive, and well-grounded in the literature *in a narrowly specified regime*: independent (or decorrelated) decisions, each reversible with bounded loss, with observable outcomes fed back quickly, aggregated in parallel rather than chained in series, with the ability to abstain. Outside that regime it is not merely overclaimed but inverted: chaining 80%-right decisions compounds *error* (0.8^10 ≈ 0.11), correlated errors defeat aggregation, any nonzero per-decision ruin probability makes ruin certain at scale, and learning from self-selected labels corrupts the signal. What actually compounds is not correctness but *calibration* (the learning loop), and that is the framing that survives scrutiny.

### Cited Findings
- Arithmetic (my own calculation from stated assumptions, not a cited result): for a *series* chain where every step must be right, P(all right) = 0.8^n: n = 2 gives 0.64; n = 3 gives 0.51; n = 5 gives 0.33; n = 10 gives 0.107; n = 20 gives 0.0115. For *parallel* independent judgments combined by majority (Condorcet), P(majority right) with p = 0.8: n = 3 gives 0.896; n = 5 gives 0.942; n = 7 gives 0.967, increasing toward 1. The same 80% produces opposite results depending only on whether decisions are chained or aggregated. — basis: [Condorcet's jury theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem)
- Arithmetic (own calculation): if each decision carries an independent probability q of an absorbing loss (ruin), survival after n decisions is (1 − q)^n; with q = 10^−4 and n = 10^6, survival ≈ e^−100 ≈ 0. Ruin that is possible per decision is certain at scale. — basis: Taleb 2018, *Skin in the Game* (ergodicity/ruin argument); [Peters 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-019-0732-0)
- Arithmetic (own calculation): with pairwise error correlation ρ among n judges, the variance of the vote share is p(1−p)[(1−ρ)/n + ρ], so as n → ∞ the variance floor is ρ·p(1−p) and the effective number of independent judges is at most 1/ρ. — basis: [Ladha 1992](https://doi.org/10.2307/2111584) (CJT under correlated votes)
- Calibration makes "80% right on average" sortable: a calibrated model's confidence partitions its decisions into high-accuracy and low-accuracy subsets, and the reject-option/selective-classification literature shows how to trade coverage for accuracy by abstaining below a confidence threshold. — [Chow 1970, "On optimum recognition error and reject tradeoff," IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 16(1):41–46](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1970.1054406); [Geifman & El-Yaniv 2017, "Selective Classification for Deep Neural Networks," NeurIPS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500)
- Kahneman and Klein: intuition is trustworthy only in high-validity environments with opportunity to learn from feedback, and confidence is not a reliable sign of accuracy. — [Kahneman & Klein 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755)
- Bezos: "70% of the information" and light-weight process are explicitly conditioned on the decision being a two-way door and on being "good at course correcting." — [Bezos 2016 letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders); [fs.blog on the 2015 letter](https://fs.blog/reversible-irreversible-decisions/)
- Popper: small interventions are valuable because their mistakes are isolable and correctable. — [SEP: Karl Popper](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/)
- Page's identity: collective error = average individual error − prediction diversity; the gain from aggregation is exactly the diversity. — Page 2007, *The Difference*
- Satopää et al.: justified extremizing of an aggregate shrinks toward zero as forecasters' information overlap grows. — [arXiv:1406.2148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.2148v2)
- Bainbridge, Parasuraman and Manzey, Elish: the human-in-the-loop on the hard 20% deskills, defers, and absorbs blame. — [Bainbridge 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8); [Parasuraman & Manzey 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055); [Elish 2019](https://doi.org/10.17351/ests2019.260)
- Ensign et al. and Shumailov et al.: training on self-selected or self-generated labels produces runaway loops and collapse. — [Ensign et al. 2018](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/ensign18a.html); [Shumailov et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07566-y)
- Hamming: what compounds is knowledge reinvested in further learning. — [Hamming 1986](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html)

### Inferences
**Steelman (when it is literally true).** Six conditions, each with a literature behind it, jointly make the thesis correct:
1. *Independence or decorrelation of errors* (Condorcet, Ladha, Page, Satopää): aggregation then drives accuracy toward 1.
2. *Reversibility with bounded loss* (Bezos, Popper, Taleb's barbell): each error is survivable and correctable, so the loss distribution has no absorbing state, and the law of large numbers applies to realized cost (ergodicity).
3. *Fast, unambiguous, outcome feedback* (Kahneman-Klein, Hogarth, Tetlock's "tracking", Wiener/Ashby): the loop closes and calibration improves; this is the true compounding term.
4. *Parallel aggregation, not serial chaining* (the 0.8^n arithmetic; Bikhchandani cascades): errors average out instead of multiplying.
5. *Calibrated confidence with the option to abstain* (Chow; selective classification; Murphy's decomposition): the 80% average becomes, e.g., 98% on the confidently-handled 60% plus escalation on the rest, so "80%" was never the operative number.
6. *Small sizing relative to the whole* (Kelly; fractional Kelly): no single decision commits enough to threaten the sequence.
Under these conditions a cheap, calibrated decider plus a threshold plus a feedback loop is not a gamble; it is a well-understood regulator, and "excellent outcomes" are a theorem-level consequence.

**Strawman (when it is dangerously false).** The same thesis is wrong, and wrong in the direction of catastrophe, when:
- Decisions are chained (routing feeds classification feeds action): 80% per step is ~11% at ten steps; the "compounding" compounds error.
- Errors are correlated through a shared model, prompt, or training set: the effective crowd size is ~1/ρ, often close to one; redundancy by re-calling the same model buys nearly nothing, and extremizing a self-correlated aggregate manufactures false confidence.
- Any decision type is a one-way door or carries ruin risk: at millions of decisions, "rare" becomes "certain."
- Feedback is delayed, missing, or produced by the system's own actions: the environment is wicked; calibration drifts, Goodhart takes over, and self-training collapses the label distribution.
- The human escalation path is nominal: the reviewer deskills, defers, and becomes the crumple zone, so the "80% automated + 20% human" system is worse than either alone.
- "80% right" is quoted as if it were a property of the model rather than of a (model, decision type, environment, period) tuple; expertise fractionates and so does machine competence.

**Where the popular version overclaims.** The "1% better every day" and "small decisions compound" rhetoric is pseudo-math: it borrows the form of compound interest without a stock to reinvest, assumes independence and no decay, and ignores that uncorrected errors persist. Munger, Bezos and Taleb are not making that claim; they are making the via-negativa claim that *avoiding ruin and staying in the game* lets ordinary averages work over long horizons. Hamming's compounding is about learning, not correctness. A software system should not inherit the pseudo-math.

**The most compelling honest framing** for a thoughtful audience, in three sentences: (1) "A calibrated cheap decider does not make 80%-good decisions; it sorts decisions into those it can make at 98% and those it should hand off, and the hand-off is the product." (2) "What compounds is not correctness but calibration: every observed outcome is reinvested in a better threshold, which is the only sense in which Hamming's compound interest applies." (3) "The system is safe exactly when its decisions are two-way doors, its errors are uncorrelated, its outcomes are observed, and no single decision can end the sequence; the engine's job is to enforce those four properties, not to assume them." That framing is defensible against every tradition surveyed here, and it turns the claimed weakness ("only 80%") into the mechanism ("calibrated enough to know which 20% to abstain on").

- **Design implication:** The "decision engine" should be built around enforcing the six steelman conditions as first-class, checkable properties of each decision type (independence measured, reversibility declared, feedback deadline set, chaining depth bounded, abstention threshold calibrated, exposure capped), and its headline metric should be *per-type calibration and abstention quality over time*, not aggregate hit-rate; marketed honestly, the product is "a decision-sorting and learning loop," not "a machine that is right 80% of the time."

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study of compounding error specifically in chained LLM-based decision pipelines that quantifies effective error correlation across calls to the same model; the arithmetic above assumes independence or a stated ρ, and the real ρ for a given model should be measured.
- No source in this search evaluates whether confidence estimates from RLHF-trained or "RLCD"-trained decision models are calibrated under distribution shift; the entire steelman depends on that empirical question.
- The Jev-specific claims ("zero hallucinations," 193.6x/444.6x) are vendor claims; I found no independent calibration or accuracy benchmark as of mid-October 2026.
