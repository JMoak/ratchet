# The mathematics and computer science of compounding many ~80%-accurate, calibrated, cheap decisions into reliable ones

Research notes, 2026-10-15. Scope: theorem-level results, empirical findings, and worked numbers at p = 0.8, organized so an engineer can implement each mechanism and a simulation that demonstrates it.

Tagging convention used throughout:
- **[THEOREM]** proven mathematical result (statement from a primary or canonical source).
- **[EMPIRICAL]** measured result from a paper; may not transfer.
- **[COMPUTED]** number I computed from the stated formula (script: `compute.py`, exact binomial / closed form, no simulation noise). Every [COMPUTED] number is reproducible from the formula next to it.

The one-paragraph answer to the motivating question: an 80%-accurate oracle can be compounded to near-certainty **if and only if its errors are (close to) independent across the calls being aggregated**, and the aggregation is *parallel* (vote / sequential-test over the same decision) or *checkpointed* (each step of a chain is independently verified and retried) rather than *serial-unchecked*. With independence, error falls exponentially in the number of calls (majority of 21 at p = 0.8 is 99.9% correct; "first to lead by 4 votes" is 99.6% correct with ~6.6 calls on average). Positive error correlation puts a hard ceiling on what any amount of aggregation can reach (pairwise correlation 0.1 among exchangeable 0.8-voters caps the infinite jury at ~97.5%; 0.3 caps it at ~87.5%). Serial dependence with no checks compounds the other way: 0.8^n, which is below 50% after 3 steps and ~1e-10 after 100. Calibration is what makes the thresholding, escalation, and Bayesian-combination mechanisms work; without it the "80%" is not an actionable number.

## Key Question 1: Parallel aggregation (Condorcet, correlated voters, weighted majority, Dawid-Skene, Hong-Page, ensemble diversity, boosting)

### Takeaway
With independent voters of competence p > 1/2, majority error decays exponentially in n (Condorcet), and at p = 0.8 you reach 99% with 13 voters, 99.9% with 21, 99.99% with 31. Positive correlation between voters' errors converts "error -> 0" into "error -> a floor set by the correlation", which is the single most important failure mode for aggregating LLM judgments; 2026 measurements on LLM judge panels find nine judges deliver about two independent votes' worth of information. When competences differ and are known, weight votes by log-odds (Nitzan-Paroush); when unknown, estimate them from agreement patterns (Dawid-Skene). Boosting is the formal proof that weak learners compound to strong ones, with training error bounded by exp(-2 sum gamma_t^2).

### Cited Findings

**Condorcet Jury Theorem (CJT)**
- [THEOREM] Statement: a group decides by majority between two outcomes, one correct; each voter independently votes correctly with probability p. If p > 1/2, adding voters increases the probability the majority is correct and it approaches 1 as n -> infinity; if p < 1/2 the optimal jury is a single voter. Juries are taken odd to avoid ties. — [Wikipedia, Condorcet's jury theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem)
- [THEOREM] Exact formula (odd n): P_n = sum_{k=(n+1)/2}^{n} C(n,k) p^k (1-p)^(n-k). Worked example on the page: n = 3, p = 0.8 gives 0.8^3 + 3(0.8^2)(0.2) = 0.896. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem)
- [THEOREM] Proof sketch (monotonicity): adding two voters changes the majority outcome only when (a) the original n were one short of a correct majority and both new voters are correct, probability (1-p)p^2, or (b) the original had exactly a bare correct majority and both new voters are wrong, probability p(1-p)^2. (a) exceeds (b) iff p > 1/2. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem)
- [THEOREM] Near p = 1/2 the gain from adding voters grows as sqrt(n): P(n,p) = 1/2 + c1 (p - 1/2) + c3 (p - 1/2)^3 + ..., c1 = C(n, floor(n/2)) (floor(n/2)+1) / 4^floor(n/2) ~ sqrt((2n+1)/pi). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem)
- Heterogeneous competences p_i: the theorem extends (Berend and Paroush; Romaniega Sancho 2022) but if the p_i are drawn from an unbiased distribution around 1/2 the asymptotic result "will not hold almost surely." Whether CJT holds in practice "is an empirical question." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem)

**Correlated voters**
- Ladha (1992), "The Condorcet Jury Theorem, free speech, and correlated votes," American Journal of Political Science 36:617-634, obtains sufficient conditions for both parts of the theorem in terms of an upper bound on the *average* positive correlation coefficient among voters; the asymptotic part holds when average covariance is small enough. — summarized in [Kaniovski and Zaigraev working paper](https://Serguei.Kaniovski.wifo.ac.at/fileadmin/pdf/condorcet_inv_corr.pdf) and [Pivato, "Epistemic democracy with correlated voters" (MPRA 69546)](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/69546/)
- Pivato (2016/2017): a suitably chosen voting rule converges to the correct answer as the population grows "even with significant voter correlation, provided average correlation shrinks as the population grows"; consistent with voters correlated through social networks or DeGroot deliberation. — [MPRA 69546](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/69546/)
- Berg (1993), "Condorcet's jury theorem, dependency among jurors," Social Choice and Welfare 10:87-95, models voting as an urn process with path-dependent probabilities (exchangeable votes). — bibliographic confirmation via [Pivato MPRA 69546](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/69546/) and [Peleg-Zamir, "Extending the Condorcet Jury Theorem to a general dependent jury"](https://hse.ru/data/2011/12/01/1270886163/Extending%20the%20Condorcet%20Jury%20Theorem%20%D0%97%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B8%D1%80.pdf)
- Kaniovski: with dependence, the "probability -> 1" half of the theorem fails unless correlation is limited; competence bounds can be computed for a given dependence structure. — [Kaniovski, correlated votes papers](https://Serguei.Kaniovski.wifo.ac.at/fileadmin/pdf/condorcet.pdf), [inv_corr](https://Serguei.Kaniovski.wifo.ac.at/fileadmin/pdf/condorcet_inv_corr.pdf)
- [EMPIRICAL, 2026] "Nine judges, two effective votes" (arXiv 2605.29800): a 9-frontier-LLM panel (7 model families) on three NLI datasets delivers about **two independent votes' worth** of information; roughly three-quarters of nominal independence is lost because models err on the same items; the panel falls **8-22 percentage points short** of what independent voting would achieve; established aggregation methods close at most 11% of the gap even when given correct answers; the best single judge matches or beats the full panel in all conditions; the deficit holds across prompt variants, temperatures, chain-of-thought, and a pairwise task (RewardBench). Uses Kish effective sample size and a Condorcet null model. — [arXiv 2605.29800](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29800)
- [EMPIRICAL, 2026] "How Many Humans Are 32 LLM Judges Worth?" (arXiv 2609.21277): a fixed 32-judge panel on three ChaosNLI tasks has human-equivalent size 2.3-3.75 (distributional-error matching) or 4.2-6.5 (spectral matching); binary-error diagnostic gives 1.97-2.23 effective votes; extrapolated asymptotes 2.39-3.99 humans, i.e. adding judges cannot push past a correlation-set ceiling. — [arXiv 2609.21277](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21277)

**Weighted majority with known competences (Nitzan-Paroush) and unknown competences (Dawid-Skene)**
- [THEOREM] Nitzan and Paroush (1982): when each expert's competence p_i is known, equiprobable alternatives and symmetric error costs, the optimal group rule is weighted majority with weights w_i = log(p_i/(1-p_i)) (log-odds); equivalent to naive Bayes. Ben-Yashar and Paroush (2001) extend to polychotomous choice. — [Berend and Kontorovich, "Consistency of weighted majority votes" (arXiv 1312.0451)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.0451); [survey arXiv 1502.02191](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.02191)
- When competences must be estimated from data, optimality is no longer automatic; Berend-Kontorovich (2013/14) analyze consistency of the plug-in rule. — [arXiv 1312.0451](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.0451)
- Dawid and Skene (1979), "Maximum likelihood estimation of observer error-rates using the EM algorithm," Applied Statistics 28(1):20-28: EM jointly estimates each labeler's confusion matrix and the latent true label from multiple noisy labels, no ground truth needed. — [JSTOR 2346806](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2346806)
- [EMPIRICAL, 2026] A Dawid-Skene-flavoured method for LLM judges: "calibrated weighted majority voting" weights each judge by online estimates of its false-positive and false-negative rates derived purely from inter-judge agreement patterns, no labels; in simulation with shifting task distributions it tracks an oracle that knows true error rates to within 0.5 pp on average and beats unweighted majority. Same paper: a judge model's task accuracy predicts its judging accuracy (Pearson r >= 0.90), and all judges are more lenient toward more capable examinees (r >= 0.83). — [arXiv 2609.12002](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12002)

**Hong-Page "diversity trumps ability" and critiques**
- Hong and Page (2004), PNAS 101(46), "Groups of diverse problem solvers can outperform groups of high-ability problem solvers." — [doi:10.1073/pnas.0403723101](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0403723101) (PNAS returned HTTP 403 to my fetch; citation from the search results)
- Thompson (2014), Notices of the AMS 61(9):1024-1030, "Does Diversity Trump Ability?": argues the diverse groups in Hong-Page's model are generated by a *random* process and that randomness, not diversity, drives the result; says the paper "does not contain information that can be applied to any real-world situation involving actual people." — reported via [The College Fix](https://www.thecollegefix.com/mathematicians-refute-oft-cited-diversity-trumps-ability-study/) and [Minding the Campus](https://mindingthecampus.org/2014/12/05/another-myth-about-diversity-crumbles/) (advocacy outlets; the primary is [Notices AMS, doi:10.1090/noti1163](https://www.ams.org/notices/201409/rnoti-p1024.pdf))
- Rebuttals/extensions: Singer, "Diversity, not randomness, trumps ability" (Philosophy of Science) defends the diversity reading; Grim et al. find "diversity does trump ability in some cases, ability trumps diversity in others," so unqualified claims are unwarranted; another Philosophy of Science paper finds the result sensitive to landscape randomness choices. — [Cambridge](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/abs/diversity-not-randomness-trumps-ability/04E08F6F5E994F2F48347BFF6DAF662E); [Grim et al. draft](https://faculty.washington.edu/conormw/Teaching/Files/FEW_2017/Grim_et_al-Expertise_versus_Diversity_across_Epistemic_Landscapes.pdf)

**Ensemble diversity decomposition (Krogh-Vedelsby)**
- Krogh and Vedelsby, "Neural Network Ensembles, Cross Validation, and Active Learning," NIPS 7 (1994/95), pp. 231-238: defines the *ambiguity* as the variation of ensemble members' outputs averaged over unlabeled data; the ambiguity decomposition (regression, squared loss) relates member error to ensemble error; ambiguity plus cross-validation estimates ensemble generalization error; ambiguity selects points for active learning. The decomposition is specific to squared loss. — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1994/hash/b8c37e33defde51cf91e1e03e51657da-Abstract.html); squared-loss restriction noted in [AAAI paper](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/10834/10834-13-14362-1-2-20201228.pdf)

**Boosting: weak learners provably compound**
- Schapire (1990), "The Strength of Weak Learnability," Machine Learning 5(2):197-227: proves weak PAC learnability (any fixed edge over 1/2) implies strong learnability (arbitrary accuracy) by combining weak hypotheses. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00116037)
- Freund and Schapire (1997), "A decision-theoretic generalization of on-line learning and an application to boosting," JCSS 55(1):119-139 (AdaBoost). — [doi:10.1006/jcss.1997.1504](https://doi.org/10.1006/jcss.1997.1504)
- [THEOREM] AdaBoost training-error bound: if round t has weighted error eps_t = 1/2 - gamma_t, the final combined classifier's training error is at most prod_t 2 sqrt(eps_t(1-eps_t)) = prod_t sqrt(1 - 4 gamma_t^2) <= exp(-2 sum_t gamma_t^2). — [Schapire, "Explaining AdaBoost"](https://rob.schapire.net/papers/explaining-adaboost.pdf); [Wikipedia AdaBoost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AdaBoost)

**How correlation destroys the gain [COMPUTED under two standard exchangeable models; see Gaps for what the primary papers say]**

Model C1, "common-signal mixture": with probability rho the entire jury copies one shared draw (correct w.p. p); otherwise voters are independent. P_n = rho p + (1 - rho) P_n^indep. The ceiling as n -> infinity is rho p + (1 - rho) = 1 - rho(1-p). At p = 0.8: rho = 0.05 caps at 0.99; 0.10 at 0.98; 0.20 at 0.96; 0.50 at 0.90. The "shared-error mass" rho(1-p) is unremovable by any number of voters.

Model C2, beta-binomial exchangeable (de Finetti): theta ~ Beta(a,b) with mean p and pairwise correlation rho = 1/(a+b+1); given theta, votes are iid Bernoulli(theta). As n -> infinity, P(majority correct) -> P(theta > 1/2), a strict ceiling below 1 for any rho > 0. At p = 0.8:

| rho | n=3 | n=9 | n=15 | n=51 | ceiling P(theta>1/2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.896 | 0.980 | 0.996 | 0.9999992 | 1 |
| 0.02 | 0.890 | 0.970 | 0.988 | 0.9994 | ~1.0000 |
| 0.05 | 0.883 | 0.955 | 0.974 | 0.992 | 0.9973 |
| 0.10 | 0.871 | 0.930 | 0.947 | 0.967 | 0.9755 |
| 0.20 | 0.849 | 0.888 | 0.899 | 0.912 | 0.9201 |
| 0.30 | 0.814 | 0.839 | 0.846 | 0.853 | 0.8753 |

Reading: at rho = 0.2, a 51-voter jury (0.912) is barely better than 9 voters (0.888) and never beats 0.92. Correlation flattens the curve rather than shifting it. This is the quantitative form of the 2026 "nine judges, two effective votes" finding.

Model C3, Chebyshev bound of the Ladha type (my derivation; Ladha's paper states the condition in terms of average correlation, see Gaps): with mean competence p and average pairwise correlation rho_bar, Var(vote share) = p(1-p)[1 + (n-1) rho_bar]/n, so P(majority wrong) <= p(1-p)[1 + (n-1) rho_bar] / (n (p-1/2)^2) -> p(1-p) rho_bar/(p-1/2)^2 as n -> infinity. At p = 0.8 the limiting bound is 1.78 rho_bar; it is vacuous (>= 1) at rho_bar >= (p-1/2)^2/(p(1-p)) = 0.5625. The asymptotic CJT (error -> 0) requires rho_bar -> 0 with n, matching Ladha/Pivato's stated condition.

Engineering reading of all three: to compound LLM votes you must *manufacture independence* (different models/families, paraphrased prompts, different temperatures/seeds, different decompositions of the question) and you must *measure* rho_bar from logs (pairwise error correlation on items with known answers, or Kish n_eff = n / (1 + (n-1) rho_bar)). With n_eff in hand, use the independent-voter table with n_eff instead of n.

**Log-odds weighting [COMPUTED]**: three voters with competences (0.9, 0.7, 0.6) have log-odds weights (2.197, 0.847, 0.405). Simple majority accuracy = 0.834 (worse than the best voter alone, 0.9); weighted majority = 0.900 (the 0.9 voter dominates, as it should because 2.197 > 0.847 + 0.405). Five voters (0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 0.6, 0.6): simple majority = 0.869; weighted = 0.896 = exactly the three 0.8 voters alone. Adding weak voters to an unweighted vote *hurts*; log-odds weighting makes them harmless. Equal competences give equal weights, so for a homogeneous 0.8 oracle, plain majority is already optimal.

**Boosting numbers at p = 0.8 (edge gamma = 0.3) [COMPUTED]**: training-error bound 0.8^T (product form) or exp(-0.18 T): T = 10 gives <= 0.107; T = 21 gives <= 0.0092; T = 30 gives <= 0.0012. The analogy to oracles is exact only if each new oracle call is trained/prompted to be good on the cases the previous ones got wrong (reweighting), which is the mechanism boosting adds beyond plain voting.

## Key Question 2: Reliability from unreliable parts (von Neumann 1956, Moore-Shannon 1956, Shannon noisy-channel coding and repetition codes)

### Takeaway
Von Neumann proved that arbitrarily reliable computation is possible from components that each fail with probability below a threshold (his majority-organ construction needs component error below about 0.0073; the later tight threshold for 3-input majority is 1/6, and 1/2 - 1/(2 sqrt(k)) for k-input gates), at the price of large redundancy. Shannon's channel-coding theorem says the "cost" of reliability need not go to infinity: any rate below capacity C = 1 - H(0.2) = 0.278 bits per use of a 20%-noisy binary channel achieves vanishing error with smart codes, whereas naive repetition (= majority vote) drives rate to zero. The oracle-aggregation analogue: majority voting is the repetition code; smarter schemes (structured decomposition, verifiers, cascades) are the "better codes."

### Cited Findings
- Von Neumann (1956), "Probabilistic Logics and the Synthesis of Reliable Organisms from Unreliable Components," in Automata Studies, Princeton, pp. 43-98. Core idea: carry each signal on a bundle of N lines and read the bit from whether most lines agree ("multiplexing"); the three-input error-correcting gate is the "majority organ," which itself fails with probability epsilon. — [IAS scan of the paper](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/Probabilistic_Logics.pdf); historical notes in [AMS Bulletin 1958 review](https://www.ams.org/bull/1958-64-03/S0002-9904-1958-10214-1/S0002-9904-1958-10214-1.pdf)
- [THEOREM] Von Neumann's majority-gate scheme works when each gate's failure probability is below about **0.0073**. — [Unger, "Noise threshold for universality of 2-input gates" (arXiv 0711.0351)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0711.0351)
- [THEOREM] Hajek and Weller (1991): reliable computation with 3-input gates is impossible at per-gate failure probability >= **1/6**; Evans-Pippenger report the 3-input threshold is exactly 1/6. — [Hajek-Weller 1991](https://hajek.ece.illinois.edu/Papers/HajekWeller91.pdf); [Evans-Pippenger TR-95-20](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/tr/1995/TR-95-20_0.pdf)
- [THEOREM] Evans and Pippenger: for formulas of noisy 2-input NAND gates the threshold is (3 - sqrt 7)/4 ~ **0.08856**; von Neumann's own NAND adaptation gives a weaker ~0.01187. Evans and Schulman give tight thresholds for odd fan-in k. The threshold for *circuits* (vs formulas) of 2-input gates remains conjectural. — [Evans-Pippenger](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/sites/default/files/tr/1995/TR-95-20_0.pdf); [Unger](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0711.0351); [Evans-Schulman](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/ewv4p-bs704/preview/EVAieeetit03.pdf)
- A reviewer's summary of von Neumann's worked estimate: with majority organs of error 1/200 and roughly 60,000-fold redundancy, a sub-network error of about 1e-20. (Secondary; not checked against the paper's text.) — search result citing [eecs.wsu.edu rb04.pdf](https://eecs.wsu.edu/~sroy/Documents/rb04.pdf)
- Moore and Shannon (1956), "Reliable Circuits Using Less Reliable Relays," Journal of the Franklin Institute 262(3):191-208 (and Part II 262(4)): builds highly reliable systems from low-reliability relays; introduces the *composition* technique where a network composed with itself repeatedly becomes much more reliable (reliability polynomial h(p) sharpened toward a step function). Described as "more encouraging" than von Neumann's gate-based approach. — [ResiliNets bibliography](https://resilinets.org/bib_moore_shannon_1956.html); [arXiv 1911.01153 (describes composition)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.01153); [Computer History Museum catalog](https://www.computerhistory.org/collections/catalog/102640577)
- [THEOREM] Binary symmetric channel with crossover probability eps has capacity C = 1 - H_b(eps) bits per use; Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem: for any rate R < C there exist codes with error probability -> 0 as block length grows, and for R > C error is bounded away from 0. — [Wikipedia, Binary symmetric channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_symmetric_channel); [Wikipedia, Noisy-channel coding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noisy-channel_coding_theorem); textbook treatment in Cover and Thomas, Elements of Information Theory, ch. 7.

### Inferences
- [COMPUTED] BSC(0.2) capacity = 1 - H(0.2) = **0.2781 bits/use**. An 80%-accurate binary oracle conveys at most 0.278 bits about the true answer per call; a single call's log-likelihood ratio is ln(0.8/0.2) = 1.386 nats = exactly **2 bits of evidence**, but the *expected* information gain is the mutual information, 0.278 bits. So you need, on average, at least 1/0.278 = 3.6 calls per reliably-decided bit, with ideal coding; repetition/majority needs more (n = 21 for 99.9%).
- [COMPUTED] Repetition code of length n over BSC(0.2) = majority of n oracle calls: n = 3 error 0.104 (rate 1/3), n = 9 error 0.0196 (rate 1/9), n = 15 error 0.0042 (rate 1/15), n = 21 error 0.00097 (rate 1/21). Rate -> 0 as error -> 0: this is the sense in which plain voting is wasteful, and why Shannon matters for the oracle question: *structured* use of calls (decompose the task so each call answers a different sub-question, then check consistency; use cheap verifiers; cascade) can in principle approach 0.278 bits/call rather than 1/n.
- [COMPUTED] Von Neumann-style restoring organ, textbook model: a 3-majority of three copies each with error e, implemented by a gate that itself flips its output with probability d, yields e' = d + (1-2d)(3e^2 - 2e^3). Iterating from e = 0: d = 0.001 -> stable error 0.00100; d = 0.0073 -> 0.00746; d = 0.01 -> 0.0103; d = 0.05 -> 0.059; d = 0.10 -> 0.146; d = 0.15 -> 0.311; d = 1/6 -> ~0.457 (the fixed point merges with 1/2, matching the Hajek-Weller threshold); d = 0.2 -> 0.5 (useless). Two lessons for the oracle problem: (i) the *aggregator* itself is a noisy component: if your vote-counter/verifier is an LLM with 20% error, the whole construction is above threshold and no amount of redundancy helps; (ii) below threshold, redundancy only drives error down to a floor of order d, not to zero, so the aggregation step must be far more reliable than the voters (use deterministic code for counting votes and comparing answers).
- The von Neumann threshold is why "80%-accurate oracles" cannot be used *as the error-correcting element* in their own compounding scheme: 0.2 > 1/6. They can be the *voters* (p = 0.8 > 1/2 is all CJT needs) provided the majority/verification logic is implemented by near-noiseless code.

### Gaps
- The 0.0073 number is confirmed only via Unger's secondary statement, not from the original text; the "1/200 error, 60,000-fold redundancy, 1e-20" example is a reviewer's paraphrase.
- Moore-Shannon's exact theorem statement (that for any relay failure probability p in (0,1) one can construct networks whose reliability function is arbitrarily close to a step function, with the step at any chosen location) is from general knowledge and the ResiliNets summary; I did not retrieve the paper.

## Key Question 3: Sequential evidence accumulation (Wald's SPRT, Bayesian log-odds updating, dependence)

### Takeaway
Because a calibrated 80% oracle call carries a log-likelihood ratio of exactly ln 4 = 1.386 nats (2 bits) for or against a binary answer, Bayesian updating is literally counting net votes: after d more "for" than "against" calls, P(correct) = 1/(1 + 0.25^d). Wald's SPRT turns this into a stopping rule: stop when the lead reaches k. At p = 0.8, k = 3 gives 98.5% with ~4.9 calls on average, k = 4 gives 99.6% with ~6.6 calls, k = 5 gives 99.9% with ~8.3 calls; a fixed-size majority needs 21 calls for 99.9%, so sequential testing cuts cost roughly 2.5x. Everything assumes independent calls; dependence must be handled by reducing the per-call evidence weight (log-odds shrinkage) or by estimating n_eff.

### Cited Findings
- [THEOREM] Wald's Sequential Probability Ratio Test: accumulate the cumulative log-likelihood ratio S_n = sum_i log[f_1(x_i)/f_0(x_i)]; accept H_1 if S_n >= A = log((1-beta)/alpha), accept H_0 if S_n <= B = log(beta/(1-alpha)), otherwise sample again; these boundary approximations (ignoring overshoot) give error rates ~alpha, beta, and the SPRT minimizes expected sample size among all tests with those error rates (Wald-Wolfowitz optimality). — [Wikipedia, SPRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_probability_ratio_test); Wald (1945), "Sequential Tests of Statistical Hypotheses," Annals of Mathematical Statistics 16(2):117-186, [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-mathematical-statistics/volume-16/issue-2/Sequential-Tests-of-Statistical-Hypotheses/10.1214/aoms/1177731118.full)
- [THEOREM] First-to-ahead-by-k voting between a correct candidate (per-sample probability p) and an alternative: P(correct wins) = p^k/(p^k + (1-p)^k) = 1/(1 + ((1-p)/p)^k), "a generalization of the gambler's ruin problem." — [MAKER paper, Section 3.2 (arXiv 2511.09030)](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09030v1); [Wikipedia, Gambler's ruin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambler%27s_ruin)
- [THEOREM] Consequence (MAKER, Appendix B): full-task success for s steps with lead-by-k voting per step is p_full = (1 + ((1-p)/p)^k)^(-s); the minimum k for target success t is k_min = ceil( ln(t^(-1/s) - 1) / ln((1-p)/p) ) = Theta(ln s), so expected cost is Theta(s ln s): "k_min grows logarithmically with s no matter the decomposition level." — [arXiv 2511.09030](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09030v1)
- [EMPIRICAL] Self-consistency's authors observe that "consistency among answers also correlates with accuracy, so it can serve as a rough confidence signal," and that performance "saturates quickly" with path count (recommend starting with 5 or 10 paths). — [Wang et al. (ar5iv 2203.11171)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.11171)

### Inferences
**Bayesian updating as log-odds addition (calibrated oracle) [THEOREM-level derivation]**: if the oracle says "A" with calibrated confidence c, the evidence it supplies is log-odds L = ln(c/(1-c)); with independent calls, posterior log-odds = prior log-odds + sum of L_i. For c = 0.8 every call contributes +/-1.386 nats, so the posterior depends only on the net vote count d: P = 1/(1 + 0.25^d). [COMPUTED] d = 1: 0.80; 2: 0.941; 3: 0.9846; 4: 0.9961; 5: 0.99902; 6: 0.99976. If the oracle's confidences vary per call (0.6 one time, 0.95 another), add the actual log-odds; a calibrated 0.95 call is worth ln 19 = 2.94 nats, more than two 0.8 calls. This is exactly the Nitzan-Paroush weighting applied per call.

The hard rule: the SPRT error guarantee is only as good as the independence assumption; verify with a held-out set by checking that observed error at each lead k matches 1/(1 + 0.25^k).

## Key Question 4: Cascades and selective prediction (Chow's rule, selective classification, model cascades, FrugalGPT and 2025-26 LLM cascades, conformal prediction)

### Takeaway
A calibrated confidence lets you *refuse to decide* on the uncertain fraction: Chow's rule says the optimal reject rule is a threshold on the maximum posterior, and the risk-coverage curve quantifies the tradeoff. Cascades monetize this: answer with the cheap 80% oracle when its confidence clears a threshold and escalate the rest to a slow strong oracle. FrugalGPT reports matching GPT-4 at up to 98% lower cost; 2025-26 work replaces raw thresholds with conformal prediction set size (distribution-free guarantee of at least 1 - K alpha coverage with K tiers) and with provable human-agreement guarantees (Trust or Escalate: >80% agreement at ~80% coverage with a 7B judge). Conformal prediction supplies the missing finite-sample guarantee: with n exchangeable calibration points, coverage lies in [1 - alpha, 1 - alpha + 1/(n+1)] with no model assumptions.

### Cited Findings
- Chow (1970), "On optimum recognition error and reject tradeoff," IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 16(1):41-46. [THEOREM, from the paper as commonly stated] Optimal rule: reject when the maximum posterior class probability falls below a threshold 1 - t; the error-reject tradeoff satisfies E(t) = -integral t dR(t) (error rate is determined by the reject curve). — [doi:10.1109/TIT.1970.1054406](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1970.1054406)
- Geifman and El-Yaniv (2017), "Selective Classification for Deep Neural Networks": a selective classifier is a pair (f, g) where g decides whether to predict; selective risk is the error on accepted points, coverage is the fraction accepted; the user sets a desired risk level and the method rejects as needed to meet it "with high probability." Headline: a 2% top-5 error on ImageNet guaranteed with 99.9% probability at nearly 60% coverage. — [arXiv 1705.08500](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500)
- Viola and Jones (2001), "Rapid Object Detection using a Boosted Cascade of Simple Features," CVPR: a cascade of increasingly complex boosted classifiers where early stages reject most negatives cheaply; the cascade's overall detection rate is the product of stage detection rates and false-positive rate the product of stage FPRs. — [CVPR 2001 paper](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~efros/courses/LBMV07/Papers/viola-cvpr-01.pdf) (the frequently quoted illustration, 10 stages each with 0.99 detection and 0.30 FP giving 0.99^10 ~ 0.90 detection and 0.30^10 ~ 6e-6 FP, is from memory of the paper; verify)
- FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia, Zou 2023): LLM pricing varies ~100x; three strategies (prompt adaptation, LLM approximation, LLM cascade). Cascade: query sent to a list of LLM APIs sequentially; a scoring function maps (query, answer) to a reliability score in [0,1] (in the HEADLINES study a DistilBERT regressor trained to predict correctness); return the answer if score >= that API's threshold, else call the next API. Optimization: maximize expected quality subject to average cost <= budget b over the LLM list and per-LLM thresholds; a mixed-integer problem, pruned by ignoring lists with small answer disagreement and approximating the objective by interpolation. Cascade length 3. HEADLINES at budget $6.5 (one-fifth of GPT-4's cost): GPT-J -> J1-L -> GPT-4 with thresholds 0.96 and 0.37; 80% cost reduction and 1.5% higher accuracy than GPT-4. Table 3 cost to match best single LLM: HEADLINES 33.1 -> 0.6 (98.3% saving), OVERRULING 9.7 -> 2.6 (73.3%), COQA 72.5 -> 29.6 (59.2%). — [arXiv 2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176); [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.05176)
- [EMPIRICAL, 2025] "Cost-Saving LLM Cascades with Early Abstention" (arXiv 2502.09054): tuning deferral *and* abstention thresholds; early abstention lowers test loss 2.2% across six benchmarks, ~13% lower cost, 5% fewer errors, at a 4.1% higher abstention rate; confidences are calibrated token probabilities; threshold optimization follows Zellinger and Thomson. — [arXiv 2502.09054](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09054v2)
- [EMPIRICAL, 2025] Zellinger and Thomson, "Rational Tuning of LLM Cascades via Probabilistic Modeling" (arXiv 2501.09345): a parametric Markov-copula model of the joint confidence distribution across the cascade's models lets thresholds be set by continuous optimization; improves area under the cost-error curve by 4.3% on average for cascades of >= 3 models and 10.2% in the low-sample regime (n <= 30 training examples). — [arXiv 2501.09345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09345)
- [EMPIRICAL, 2025] "Semantic Agreement for Open-Ended LLM Cascades" (arXiv 2509.21837): agreement among ensemble outputs replaces token-level confidence as the deferral signal; reaches target-model quality at ~40% of cost across 500M-70B models. — [arXiv 2509.21837](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21837v1)
- [EMPIRICAL, 2025] "Bi-directional Model Cascading with Proxy Confidence" (arXiv 2504.19391): uses both the small model's confidence and a cheap proxy estimate of the large model's confidence; fewer deferrals on multiple-choice datasets. — [mirror](https://sinoxiv.napstic.cn/article/24577867)
- [THEOREM+EMPIRICAL, 2026] "Conformal Cascade" (arXiv 2607.25018): deferral rule = conformal prediction set size; accept when the calibrated set "collapses to a single answer," else defer to the next tier. Guarantee: a per-tier union bound gives coverage >= 1 - K alpha at the accepting tier for K tiers; under a "selection-preservation" condition it tightens to 1 - alpha. No training, black-box API access only. 18 multiple-choice benchmarks, two-tier cascades from four open-weight families; strictly improves over the strongest calibration-tuned heuristic cascade on most pairs, largest gains where majority vote is unreliable; on easy benchmarks the vast majority of queries stay with the small model at no accuracy cost. Open-ended generation needs an answer-clustering step (future work). — [arXiv 2607.25018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25018)
- [THEOREM+EMPIRICAL, 2024] "Trust or Escalate: LLM Judges with Provable Guarantees for Human Agreement" (Jung, Brahman, Choi, arXiv 2407.18370): selective evaluation estimates judge confidence and only trusts the judgment when confident; agreement with humans is provably guaranteed at a user-specified level; "Simulated Annotators" improve calibration; "Cascaded Selective Evaluation" uses cheaper judges first and stronger ones only when needed with the same guarantee. On a Chatbot Arena subset where GPT-4 almost never reaches 80% human agreement, the method with Mistral-7B "guarantees over 80% human agreement with almost 80% test coverage." — [arXiv 2407.18370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370)

- [2026] "Localize-Then-Decide": conformal prediction first shortlists candidate responses containing the human-preferred one with high probability, then a calibrated confidence rule selects or abstains; argues plain confidence thresholding "breaks down as the number of candidates grows." — [arXiv 2608.25824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25824)
- Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification": conformal prediction wraps any pre-trained model with prediction sets that "possess explicit, non-asymptotic guarantees even without distributional assumptions or model assumptions," guaranteed to contain the truth with user-specified probability; covers structured outputs, distribution shift, time series, outliers, and abstaining models. — [arXiv 2107.07511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511)

### Inferences
- [THEOREM, split conformal recipe from the tutorial; stated from knowledge, verify against arXiv 2107.07511 Section 1] Given n exchangeable calibration points with conformity scores s_i = 1 - p_hat(true label), set q_hat = the ceil((n+1)(1-alpha))/n empirical quantile of the s_i; the prediction set C(x) = {y : 1 - p_hat_y(x) <= q_hat} satisfies 1 - alpha <= P(Y in C(X)) <= 1 - alpha + 1/(n+1). [COMPUTED] alpha = 0.1: n = 100 -> use the 91st smallest score, coverage in [0.90, 0.9099]; n = 1000 -> 901st, [0.90, 0.9010]; n = 10,000 -> 9001st, [0.90, 0.9001]. This is the *distribution-free* replacement for "trust the 0.8": it needs no calibration of the scores, only exchangeability, and the guarantee is marginal (averaged over items), not per-item.
- Cascade cost-accuracy arithmetic [COMPUTED, toy]: a cheap oracle whose calibrated confidences are 0.6 on 30% of items, 0.8 on 30%, 0.95 on 40% (overall 80%) with a strong oracle at 98% accuracy and 10x cost. Escalate nothing: acc 0.80, cost 1. Escalate conf < 0.7: acc 0.914, cost 4. Escalate conf < 0.9: acc 0.968, cost 7. Escalate all: acc 0.98, cost 11 (worse than strong-only, because you paid for the cheap call too). General formula: acc = sum over confidence buckets w_b [c_b if c_b >= tau else a_strong]; cost = c_cheap + c_strong * P(conf < tau). The optimal tau is where the marginal accuracy gain per unit cost, (a_strong - c_b)/c_strong, drops below your value-per-correct-answer, which is a Lagrangian threshold: escalate bucket b iff (a_strong - c_b) * V >= c_strong. Calibration is load-bearing here: if the "0.95" bucket is really 0.85, the cascade silently under-escalates.
- Selective prediction at p = 0.8 with the same buckets: keep only conf >= 0.9 (40% coverage) -> selective risk 5%; keep conf >= 0.7 (70% coverage) -> risk 1 - (0.24+0.38)/0.7 = 11.4%. The risk-coverage curve is the thing to plot from logs, and Geifman-El-Yaniv's SGR procedure gives a high-probability bound on the selected risk from a held-out set.
- Why the Chow threshold and the Bayes threshold are the same object: rejecting is just a third action with its own cost; the optimal rule is "reject iff expected loss of the best action exceeds the reject cost," which with symmetric 0-1 loss is "reject iff max posterior < 1 - (reject cost)." See KQ8 for the asymmetric-cost version.

## Key Question 5: Calibration itself (definitions, Brier decomposition, reliability diagrams, Platt and temperature scaling, verification from logs)

### Takeaway
"Calibrated" means P(correct | stated confidence = c) = c. It is the property that makes every other mechanism in these notes legal: Bayesian log-odds addition, SPRT thresholds, Chow/cascade thresholds, Kelly sizing, and expected-value decisions all consume the stated probability as a probability. Modern neural nets are badly miscalibrated out of the box (ECE 16.5% for ResNet-110 on CIFAR-100) and a single-parameter temperature fixes most of it (to 1.26%) without changing any argmax decision. Calibration is verifiable from outcome logs by binning (ECE, reliability diagram) or by the Brier decomposition; a bin needs roughly 250-400 outcomes to pin its accuracy within +/-5%.

### Cited Findings
- [THEOREM/definition] Perfect calibration: P(Y_hat = Y | P_hat = p) = p for all p in [0,1]. — [Guo et al. 2017 (ar5iv 1706.04599)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1706.04599)
- [definition] Expected Calibration Error with M bins B_m over n samples: ECE = sum_{m=1}^{M} (|B_m|/n) |acc(B_m) - conf(B_m)|; Maximum Calibration Error MCE = max_m |acc(B_m) - conf(B_m)|; the paper uses M = 15. Reliability diagrams plot expected sample accuracy as a function of confidence; a perfectly calibrated model lies on the diagonal; the diagram does not show bin counts. — [Guo et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1706.04599)
- [EMPIRICAL] Modern networks are poorly calibrated; depth, width, weight decay and Batch Normalization influence calibration. — [Guo et al., ICML 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html)
- [method] Temperature scaling: q_hat = max_k softmax(z/T)_k with a single scalar T > 0 fit by minimizing NLL on a validation set; T does not change the argmax so "temperature scaling does not affect the model's accuracy." Single-parameter variant of Platt scaling. ECE (M = 15) before -> after: CIFAR-100 ResNet-110 16.53% -> 1.26%; CIFAR-100 DenseNet-40 10.37% -> 1.18%; ImageNet DenseNet-161 6.28% -> 1.99%; ImageNet ResNet-152 5.48% -> 1.86%. — [Guo et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1706.04599)
- [THEOREM] Brier score BS = (1/N) sum (f_t - o_t)^2 for probability forecasts f_t and outcomes o_t in {0,1}; Murphy (1973) decomposition BS = Reliability - Resolution + Uncertainty, where Reliability = sum_k (n_k/N)(f_k - o_bar_k)^2 (calibration term, 0 is perfect), Resolution = sum_k (n_k/N)(o_bar_k - o_bar)^2 (how much forecasts separate outcomes), Uncertainty = o_bar(1 - o_bar). — [Wikipedia, Brier score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brier_score); Murphy (1973), "A new vector partition of the probability score," J. Applied Meteorology 12:595-600, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2)
- Platt (1999), "Probabilistic outputs for support vector machines...": fit a logistic sigmoid P(y=1|f) = 1/(1 + exp(a f + b)) to held-out scores. — cited as the parent of temperature scaling in [Guo et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html)
- [EMPIRICAL] LLM self-consistency: agreement rate among sampled answers "correlates with accuracy, so it can serve as a rough confidence signal." — [Wang et al. (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.11171)
- [EMPIRICAL] Judge confidence can be calibrated well enough to give provable human-agreement guarantees ("Simulated Annotators"). — [Trust or Escalate, arXiv 2407.18370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370)

### Inferences
- [COMPUTED] A perfectly calibrated oracle that always says 0.8 and is right 80% of the time has Brier score 0.8(0.2)^2 + 0.2(0.8)^2 = 0.16 = Uncertainty, with Reliability 0 and Resolution 0. Such an oracle is calibrated but *useless for selective prediction*: there is no uncertain subset to escalate. The value of calibration for cascades comes from calibration *plus resolution*: the oracle must say 0.6 on the items it gets wrong more often and 0.95 on the ones it nails. So the two numbers to log and track are ECE (or Reliability) and Resolution (or AUROC of confidence vs correctness).
- Why calibration enables thresholding: Chow/cascade rules act on P(correct | confidence). If the oracle is miscalibrated by a monotone distortion (e.g. overconfident), the *ordering* is still fine and a threshold tuned on held-out outcomes still works (this is what Platt/temperature scaling fixes and why FrugalGPT learns per-model thresholds empirically). If miscalibration is non-monotone (confidence 0.9 is less accurate than confidence 0.7 on some slice), no threshold works and you need per-slice recalibration or conformal sets.
- Why calibration enables compounding: log-odds addition is only valid if each input probability is a probability. Feeding overconfident 0.95s into the Bayesian update when the true hit rate is 0.8 inflates the posterior by exp(ln 19 - ln 4) = 4.75x in odds per call; after four calls you believe 99.99% when the truth is 99.6%, and the SPRT will stop early with higher real error than nominal.
- [COMPUTED] Verifying calibration from outcome logs: a reliability bin's empirical accuracy has 95% CI half-width 1.96 sqrt(c(1-c)/n_bin); for +/-0.10 you need ~62 outcomes per bin at c = 0.8 (97 worst case), for +/-0.05 ~246 (385 worst case), for +/-0.02 ~1537 (2401). Practical procedure: (1) log (stated confidence, outcome) for every decision whose truth later becomes known; (2) bin into 10-15 confidence bins (or use adaptive equal-mass bins); (3) compute ECE and plot the reliability diagram with Wilson intervals; (4) compute the Murphy decomposition; (5) fit a temperature or Platt map on half the log and check ECE on the other half; (6) re-check per slice (task type, length, model version) because aggregate calibration can hide slice miscalibration. Treat any judge's "0.8" as unverified until step 3 has been done on >= ~250 outcomes per bin.

### Gaps
- No LLM-specific primary source was retrieved that measures the calibration (ECE) of verbalized confidences of current frontier models at scale; the 2024-26 cascade papers use token probabilities or agreement rates and note their shortcomings rather than reporting ECE tables.

## Key Question 6: LLM-specific evidence (self-consistency, best-of-N with verifiers, test-time compute scaling, LLM-as-judge reliability, Mixture-of-Agents, debate, 2025-26 small-model compounding)

### Takeaway
Empirically, sampling-and-voting (self-consistency) buys ~10-18 accuracy points on math benchmarks and saturates within 5-40 samples; verifier-based best-of-N keeps improving to N ~ 1000+ when the verifier is good (PRM 78.2% vs majority 69.6% at N = 1860), but verifiers can be gamed at large N (Cobbe: performance falls past 400 samples). *Coverage* (any sample correct) scales log-linearly over four orders of magnitude, but majority voting and reward models plateau around 100-several hundred samples, so without a reliable verifier the gap between "some sample is right" and "we can pick it" is the whole problem. LLM judges reach ~85% agreement with humans (same as human-human) but have severe position bias (Claude-v1 consistency 23.8%, GPT-4 65%) and verbosity bias; panels of judges are cheaper and less biased than one big judge but share correlated errors (two effective votes from nine). Debate raises weak-judge accuracy (LLM judge 48% -> 76%; humans 60% -> 88% on QuALITY) but the DeepMind replication finds more modest gains. MAKER (Nov 2025) is the existence proof: a million-step task completed with zero errors using microagents + first-to-ahead-by-k voting + red-flagging, with cost Theta(s ln s).

### Cited Findings

**Self-consistency / majority voting**
- [EMPIRICAL] Wang et al. (2022): sample diverse reasoning paths and take the most consistent final answer. Gains over greedy CoT: GSM8K +17.9%, SVAMP +11.0%, AQuA +12.2%, StrategyQA +6.4%, ARC-challenge +3.9%. GSM8K: PaLM-540B 56.5 -> 74.4; code-davinci-002 60.1 -> 78.0; LaMDA-137B 17.1 -> 27.7. 40 samples per run averaged over 10 runs; temperature 0.7 top-k 40 (PaLM), 0.5/40 (UL2, LaMDA), 0.7 no top-k (GPT-3). Hypothesis: "correct reasoning processes, even if they are diverse, tend to have greater agreement in their final answer than incorrect processes." — [arXiv 2203.11171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171); [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.11171)

### Inferences
- The empirical record lines up with the theory in KQ1-3: self-consistency saturating at 5-40 samples is the correlated-voter ceiling (same model, same prompt, errors correlated), not the independent-voter curve (which would keep improving). The fix the literature converges on is an *independent verifier* (tests, proofs, PRM), which breaks the correlation between generator and selector, and the failure mode is the verifier being gamed at large N (Cobbe > 400 samples; Snell's beam-search over-optimization), which is the "noisy aggregator above threshold" failure from von Neumann.
- Zheng's position-bias numbers are a calibration-of-the-judge problem: a judge whose verdict flips 35-76% of the time when order is swapped is not a p = 0.8 oracle on that item; the swap-and-tie mitigation is a two-vote agreement rule (accept only if both orderings agree), which under independence would turn p = 0.8 into 0.941 but under the measured correlation turns mostly into abstentions.
- MAKER's result is consistent with the lead-by-k math at a very high per-step p (0.9978): with k = 3, per-step success 1/(1 + (0.0022/0.9978)^3) ~ 1 - 1.1e-8, so over 1.05M steps expected failures ~0.011; k = 3 was indeed sufficient. [COMPUTED] At p = 0.8 the same scheme needs k = 10 for a 1e-6 per-step error (16.6 calls/step on average); for a 1000-step chain at 95% overall you need per-step error <= 5e-5, i.e. k = 8 (13.3 calls/step), so ~13,300 oracle calls for 1,000 decisions. The s ln s law holds; the constant is ~13 at p = 0.8 vs ~3 at p = 0.998, so a 0.8 oracle is usable but roughly 4-5x more expensive per decision than a 0.998 one at the same end-to-end reliability, *if* errors are independent and each step is a clean binary/clusterable decision.

## Key Question 7: Sequential error compounding and how to break the chain (0.8^n, pass^k, METR horizons, compounding analyses, verification checkpoints, retries, ratcheting, verifier math)

### Takeaway
A chain of n unchecked dependent steps at p = 0.8 succeeds with probability 0.8^n: 51% at 3 steps, 11% at 10, 1e-10 at 100; the 50% horizon is H = ln 0.5 / ln p = 3.1 steps at p = 0.8, 69 at p = 0.99, 693 at p = 0.999, and tiny per-step improvements translate into hyperbolically longer horizons. Empirically, agents' per-step reliability saturates below 1 and degrades with their own accumulated errors in context ("self-conditioning"), so real chains decay at least geometrically; METR measures a 50%-time-horizon doubling every ~7 months with the 80% horizon 4-6x shorter. The chain is broken by inserting a verifier after each step and resampling on rejection: the per-step success becomes p(1-f) / [p(1-f) + (1-p)(1-t)] where t = verifier true-positive (catch) rate and f = false-alarm rate; a verifier that is itself only 80% accurate lifts a 0.8 step to 0.941 (not enough for long chains), a 99%/1% verifier lifts it to 0.9975 (100 steps at 78%). Ratcheting (lock in verified progress so a later failure costs only a local retry) converts the product into a sum of per-step retry costs.

### Cited Findings
- [EMPIRICAL] tau-bench (Yao et al. 2024): simulated user + agent with API tools and policy; success = final database state matches goal; proposes pass^k ("reliability over multiple trials"); SOTA function-calling agents incl. GPT-4o succeed on < 50% of tasks and are inconsistent: pass^8 < 25% in retail. — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)
- [EMPIRICAL] METR (2025), "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks": the 50% time horizon is the human-time length of tasks a model completes 50% of the time; success modeled as p = sigma((log h_agent - log t_task) beta_agent); horizon doubled every 207 days (95% CI 166-240) since 2019, ~7 months; o3's 50% horizon ~110 minutes, GPT-2's ~2 seconds; the **80% horizon is 4-6x shorter** than the 50% horizon and has a similar doubling time (204 days); progress attributed to greater reliability, better adaptation to mistakes, logical reasoning and tool use; common failure types include repeating failed actions and premature abandonment; exponential fit of success vs human task length R^2 ~ 0.80. — [arXiv 2503.14499](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v3)

**Verifier math [derived; standard]**: a step produces a correct output w.p. p; a verifier rejects wrong outputs w.p. t (true-positive/catch rate) and wrongly rejects correct outputs w.p. f (false-alarm rate). Resample until accepted. Then
- P(accepted output is correct) = q = p(1-f) / [p(1-f) + (1-p)(1-t)]
- E[attempts per step] = 1 / [p(1-f) + (1-p)(1-t)]
- n-step chain success = q^n (if verifier errors are independent across steps and resamples).

Readings: (i) the verifier's *catch rate t* is what matters for reliability; its false-alarm rate f only costs retries (f = 0.2 vs 0.05 at t = 0.95 changes q from 0.987 to 0.985 but attempts from 1.30 to 1.54). So prefer strict verifiers. (ii) An 80%-accurate LLM verifying an 80%-accurate LLM gives 0.941 per step, identical to requiring two independent agreeing votes (posterior 1/(1+0.25^2)), and is nowhere near enough for long chains (100 steps: 0.2%). (iii) Two independent 80% verifiers that must both accept give t_eff = 0.96, f_eff = 0.36, q = 0.9846, 1.9 attempts/step: three 80% oracles per step (one proposer + two checkers) buys 98.5% per step. (iv) Deterministic checks (tests, type checks, schema validation, re-execution) have f ~ 0 and whatever t their coverage gives; a check that catches half the errors with no false alarms gives 0.889. Stack independent deterministic checks: t_total = 1 - prod (1 - t_i). (v) Finite retry budget R with p = 0.8, t = f = 0.8/0.2: R = 1 -> correct 0.64, accepted-wrong 0.04, exhausted 0.32; R = 3 -> 0.910 / 0.057 / 0.033; R = 5 -> 0.938 / 0.059 / 0.003. Accepted-wrong converges to 0.0588 = 1 - q; the retry budget only trades "exhausted" for "correct."

**Mechanisms that break the chain, with their math**:
1. *Verification checkpoints*: as above; end-to-end goes from p^n to q^n with q set by the verifier's catch rate. Required per-step q for n steps at target T is T^(1/n): 100 steps at 95% needs q >= 0.99949; 1000 steps needs 0.999949. With first-to-ahead-by-k on each step instead of a verifier, k = 7-8 at p = 0.8 (KQ3 table).
2. *Independent resampling on retry*: the formula assumes the retry is a fresh draw. Sinha et al.'s self-conditioning result says a retry *in the same context with the error visible* is not a fresh draw (accuracy is lower after seeing own errors); MAKER's red-flagging discards the bad output before resampling and uses fresh microagent contexts. Implementation rule: retry from the last verified state with the failed attempt removed from context, optionally with a paraphrased prompt or different model to decorrelate.
3. *Reversibility*: if a wrong step can be undone at cost c_undo, the expected cost of a step is bounded and the chain's success is no longer a product: you can afford detection *after* the fact. Irreversible steps (sent email, deleted data, executed trade) must have the highest q, which argues for spending the verification budget unevenly, concentrating votes/verifiers on irreversible steps. (Design inference; no source found that formalizes this for LLM agents.)
4. *Ratcheting*: lock in each verified step (commit, snapshot, test suite green) so a later failure only requires redoing steps since the last ratchet. With ratchets every m steps and per-step q, the expected total work is (n/m) * E[attempts to get m consecutive accepted-good steps] ~ (n/m) / q^m, versus 1/q^n for an all-or-nothing chain. For q = 0.9412 (80% oracle + 80% verifier), n = 100: unratcheted expected restarts 1/q^100 = 430; ratchet every step: 100 * 1.06 = 106 step-attempts. Ratcheting is what turns exponential cost into linear cost; the verifier is what makes the ratchet trustworthy (a ratchet locks in whatever passed, including accepted-wrong outputs at rate 1 - q, so those compound: after n ratcheted steps P(no latent accepted-wrong) = q^n still; ratcheting fixes cost, not correctness).
5. *Decomposition into clusterable decisions*: MAKER's maximal decomposition makes each step a small, checkable, votable unit; the theory (lead-by-k) requires votes to be comparable, which free-form multi-step outputs are not. The s ln s cost law needs m = 1 (one decision per vote). Decomposition is what makes parallel aggregation (KQ1-3) applicable to a sequential task.

## Key Question 8: Decision-theoretic framing (expected value with asymmetric costs, optimal threshold, value of information, Kelly and ruin)

### Takeaway
With a calibrated probability q of being right and costs C_FP (acting wrongly) and C_FN (failing to act when you should), act iff q > C_FP/(C_FP + C_FN); the 80% oracle is good enough to act on alone only when the cost ratio is mild (threshold 0.5 at 1:1, 0.909 at 10:1 against acting). The value of buying one more 80% vote is zero if you will act the same way regardless, and positive only when disagreement would change the action, which with a 0.8 prior means you need an abstain/escalate option for the extra vote to pay. Kelly says compounding bets on an 80% edge at even odds should stake 60% of bankroll for 19.3% log-growth per bet, and staking more than ~93% has negative growth with certain ruin at 100%: overconfidence (miscalibration) is the overbetting failure mode.

### Cited Findings
- [THEOREM] Cost-sensitive optimal decision: for binary decisions with cost matrix C(i,j) (cost of predicting i when truth is j), predict positive iff p(1|x) C(1,0) + ... i.e. the optimal threshold is p* = (C(1,0) - C(0,0)) / ((C(1,0) - C(0,0)) + (C(0,1) - C(1,1))); with zero cost for correct decisions this is C_FP/(C_FP + C_FN). — Elkan (2001), "The Foundations of Cost-Sensitive Learning," [IJCAI paper](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~elkan/rescale.pdf)
- [THEOREM] Kelly criterion: for a bet that wins b-to-1 with probability p, the growth-optimal fraction of bankroll is f* = p - (1-p)/b (= 2p - 1 at even odds); expected log-growth g(f) = p ln(1 + bf) + (1-p) ln(1 - f); betting above f* lowers growth, and above the point where g = 0 the bankroll shrinks almost surely; fractional Kelly trades growth for variance. — [Wikipedia, Kelly criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_criterion); Kelly (1956), "A New Interpretation of Information Rate," Bell System Technical Journal 35:917-926.
- [THEOREM] Expected value of information (Howard 1966): EVPI = E[max_a U(a, theta)] - max_a E[U(a, theta)] >= 0; the value of *sample* information from an imperfect signal is computed by preposterior analysis, averaging the post-signal optimal decision over the signal distribution; information has value only if it can change the decision. — [Wikipedia, Value of information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_of_information)
- [THEOREM] Chow's reject option is the three-action special case of the same framework (KQ4). — [Chow 1970](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1970.1054406)
- [EMPIRICAL] Cascade papers that make the escalation decision explicitly cost-aware: FrugalGPT (maximize quality s.t. average cost <= budget) and Zellinger-Thomson (continuous optimization of thresholds against a probabilistic model). — [arXiv 2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176); [arXiv 2501.09345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09345)

### Inferences
- [COMPUTED] Thresholds: C_FP:C_FN = 1:1 -> act iff q > 0.50; 1:10 (missing is 10x worse) -> 0.091; 10:1 (acting wrongly is 10x worse) -> 0.909; 1:100 -> 0.0099. So a single 0.8-confident oracle call justifies acting when a false positive costs less than 4x a false negative (threshold 0.8 at ratio 4:1); for anything more asymmetric you must compound first. Compounding target from the threshold: to act when C_FP/C_FN = r you need posterior q > r/(1+r), i.e. net votes d with 1/(1 + 0.25^d) > r/(1+r), d > log_4 r. r = 10 -> d >= 2 (0.941); r = 100 -> d >= 4 (0.996); r = 1000 -> d >= 5 (0.999).
- [COMPUTED] Value of one more 80% vote when the prior is 0.8 for action A and the cost of a wrong action is 1: P(agree) = 0.68, posterior 0.941; P(disagree) = 0.32, posterior 0.5. If you must act either way, expected loss after the vote is 0.68(0.059) + 0.32(0.5) = 0.200, identical to before: **VOI = 0**, because at posterior 0.5 you still act on A (tie-break) and at 0.941 you act on A. The vote only has value if disagreement triggers a different action: with an abstain/escalate option costing 0.1, expected loss becomes 0.68(0.059) + 0.32(0.1) = 0.072, so VOI = 0.128 per vote. General rule: buy another cheap decision only if some outcome of it crosses a decision threshold (Elkan's p*, or the escalate threshold); otherwise escalate directly or act. This is the decision-theoretic reason cascades need the *option* to escalate and why SPRT's two boundaries (not one) matter.
- When to buy another cheap vote vs escalate to a strong oracle (cost c_s, accuracy a_s) vs act now: compare (a) act: loss (1 - q) C; (b) keep buying cheap votes at c_c each with lead-by-k stopping, then act; (c) escalate: c_s + (1 - a_s) C. With q = 0.8, C = 1, a_s = 0.98: (a) 0.20; (c) c_s + 0.02; so escalating beats acting now iff c_s < 0.18 C. Lead-by-4 voting costs ~6.6 c_c and leaves error 0.0039, so [COMPUTED] voting beats escalation iff 6.6 c_c + 0.0039 C < c_s + (1 - a_s) C. At a 10x price ratio (c_s = 10 c_c) and a_s = 0.98 this holds for every C: *independent* cheap votes are both cheaper (6.6 vs 10 calls) and more accurate (0.996 vs 0.98) than one strong call. What reverses it is correlation, not price: under the beta-binomial model with rho = 0.1 the cheap oracle's ceiling is 0.9755 (KQ1), so no number of cheap votes reaches the strong oracle's 0.98 and escalation is the only route below 2.4% error. The pivotal input to the buy-vs-escalate decision is therefore the measured n_eff / correlation ceiling of the cheap oracle, and the decision rule is: buy cheap votes while the ceiling exceeds the accuracy you need and the marginal vote still crosses a decision threshold; escalate once the target accuracy exceeds the ceiling.
- [COMPUTED] Kelly at p = 0.8, even odds: f* = 0.6; growth per bet g(0.6) = 0.8 ln 1.6 + 0.2 ln 0.4 = 0.1927 nats (x1.213 per bet, doubling every 3.6 bets). Half-Kelly f = 0.3: g = 0.139 (x1.149, doubling in 5.0 bets) with far lower variance. f = 0.8: g = 0.148; f = 0.9: g = 0.053; f = 0.95: g = -0.065 (shrinking); f = 1.0: ruin on the first loss (20% chance per bet, certain eventually). The "ruin from overbetting" result maps directly onto miscalibration: if the oracle says 0.8 but is really 0.6, then f* is 0.2 but you stake 0.6, and g(0.6 | p = 0.6) = 0.6 ln 1.6 + 0.4 ln 0.4 = -0.085 < 0: a bankroll that shrinks almost surely while every individual bet looks positive-EV. Calibration verification (KQ5) is the prerequisite for any compounding-bets strategy; fractional Kelly is the hedge against residual miscalibration.
- Expected value of a compounded decision: if n independent 0.8 votes produce a majority verdict with error e_n (KQ1 table) and acting on it yields gain G if right and loss L if wrong, EV = (1 - e_n) G - e_n L - n c_c; the optimal n maximizes this, and since e_n falls exponentially in n while cost rises linearly, the optimum is where the marginal error reduction times (G + L) equals the cost of two more votes; in practice evaluate the KQ1 table directly: at L = 100 c_c, G = 10 c_c, n = 1 gives EV = 8 - 20 - 1 = -13 (do not act on one vote); n = 7 gives 9.67 - 3.33 - 7 = -0.67; n = 9 gives 9.80 - 1.96 - 9 = -1.2; the decision is never worth it at these prices with fixed-n voting, but with lead-by-k (k = 4, 6.6 calls, error 0.0039) EV = 9.96 - 0.39 - 6.6 = +3.0. Sequential testing is what makes marginal decisions worth compounding.

## Implementation and simulation checklist (synthesis, for the engineer)

1. **Measure the oracle**: on a labeled set, log (confidence, correct?) and compute accuracy, ECE (15 bins), Brier decomposition, and the risk-coverage curve. Recalibrate with temperature/Platt if ECE > ~0.03. Nothing below is valid until this is done (KQ5).
2. **Measure independence**: on the same items, draw m repeated calls and compute pairwise error correlation rho_bar and n_eff = n/(1 + (n-1) rho_bar), or directly fit the beta-binomial rho from the distribution of #correct out of m. Compare observed majority-of-m accuracy to the independent-voter table; the gap is the correlation tax (KQ1). Try decorrelators (model family, paraphrase, temperature, decomposition) and re-measure.
3. **Parallel decisions**: use log-odds-weighted voting with weights from measured per-source accuracy (Nitzan-Paroush) or estimated via Dawid-Skene/agreement-based EM when labels are scarce (KQ1). Prefer lead-by-k sequential stopping over fixed n (k = 4 for 99.6%, k = 5 for 99.9% at p = 0.8 if independent; use n_eff-adjusted log-odds otherwise) (KQ3).
4. **Selective/cascade**: put a conformal or empirically tuned threshold on the cheap oracle; escalate the rest; choose the threshold by the Lagrangian rule escalate iff (a_strong - c_bucket) V >= c_strong (KQ4, KQ8). Report coverage and selective risk, not just accuracy.
5. **Sequential tasks**: decompose into single checkable decisions; after each, run the strictest verifier you have (deterministic first, then independent model-based), resample from a clean context on rejection, and ratchet verified state. Track per-step q and compute q^n; budget verification toward irreversible steps (KQ7).
6. **Simulation to demonstrate each mechanism**: Bernoulli(0.8) voters with a tunable exchangeable correlation (mixture or beta-binomial) to reproduce the KQ1 tables; a lead-by-k random walk to reproduce the KQ3 table and its latency tail; a chain of n steps with a (t, f) verifier to reproduce the KQ7 table; a Kelly bankroll with true p != stated p to show ruin from miscalibration (KQ8). All formulas above are closed-form, so the simulation can be checked against them exactly.
