# Calibrated decisions compound; chained decisions collapse

The claim that "decision making at an 80% good rate is effectively compoundable" is sound in exactly one regime and inverted everywhere else, and TypeSafe's Jev is a workable but still unproven instance of the component that regime requires. Jev is a hosted, text-first, non-generative model that answers typed questions (yes/no "Noul", "Choice" over up to 255 options, "Score" over 2 to 10 levels) about one shared `state` in a single roughly 100 to 500 ms request at **$0.042 per million input tokens with output free**, returning probability distributions plus a server-derived `confidence` that is a deterministic shape statistic, not a correctness estimate ([TypeSafe API reference](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api); [Confidence](https://docs.typesafe.ai/confidence)). On independent measurement it ranks its own errors well (AUROC near 0.9) and sits **3 to 7 points below frontier LLMs on bounded classification** (81% against 84.4% on Banking77; 67.8% against 74.1% on TypeSafe's own four workflows) while trailing badly on arithmetic, dates, multi-hop reasoning and legal entailment; its calibration is **not absolute per task** (ECE from 0.011 to 0.44 across independent tests, with base-rate shifts that move the F1-optimal threshold to about 0.35), and it is steerable by plausible evidence (a 59% prompt-injection break rate; 61 to 73% of correct decisions flipped by benign-looking context) ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/independent-evaluation-of-jev); [arXiv 2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29429); [Check Point](https://blog.checkpoint.com/ai-security/jev-is-not-a-language-model-but-it-breaks-like-one-prompt-injection-against-a-typed-decision-model/); [arXiv 2609.30243](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30243)). The mathematics is unambiguous: independent 80% votes reach 99.9% with 21 parallel votes or about 8.3 sequential ones, but pairwise error correlation of 0.1 caps any jury at 97.5% and 0.3 caps it at 87.5%; an unchecked chain decays as 0.8^n, which is 11% at ten steps; and it is a verifier's catch rate, not the voter's accuracy, that rescues a chain. The decision-theoretic and philosophical literature (Condorcet, Chow, Kelly, Ashby, Kahneman and Klein, Bainbridge) has real substance, but what it says compounds is calibration through closed feedback, not correctness; the popular "compounding" rhetoric is pseudo-math. Every ingredient of a compounding engine already exists somewhere (DMN hit policies, OPA decision logs, bandit apprentice mode, guarded rollouts, FrugalGPT cascades, DSPy's ReAnchor, Temporal event histories, NannyML's label-free accuracy estimation), and no system joins a probability to a threshold version, a decision mode and a later outcome, which is the gap. The engine must therefore enforce rather than assume seven properties: measured independence, declared reversibility, deterministic floors that only raise caution, verification at checkpoints, shadow by default, learning only from rows the engine did not select, and abstention as a first-class result.

## Jev is a $0.042-per-million typed oracle that ranks well but is not calibrated per task

### One request, many independent questions, probabilities out

Jev launched on 15 September 2026 as TypeSafe AI's first "System One Model", in waitlisted early access alongside a $40M DCVC seed; signups opened to everyone on 20 September and were paused on 22 September "to ensure quality of service", and whether direct signups have reopened is disputed across third-party sources while Vercel's AI Gateway and OpenRouter have never gated it ([Launch blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev); [flaviocopes](https://flaviocopes.com/jev-api-key/)). On 9 October TypeSafe announced an **$870M Series A at a $7.5B valuation** led by Andreessen Horowitz and claimed roughly a third of the Fortune 500 as users without naming any ([Series A post](https://typesafe.ai/blog/series-ai)). The model is proprietary, hosted only, US-processed, and TypeSafe publishes no architecture, parameter count, weights, training data or technical paper; the only description of its training is a name, "Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions", with the stated goal that "outcomes assigned a probability of 0.2 should occur about 20% of the time" ([AI primer](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction/machine-learning-primer); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jev_(AI_model))).

The entire API is one endpoint, `POST https://api.typesafe.ai/v1/systemone` with a Bearer key, taking `{state, model, questions}` where `state` is a string, object or array and `questions` is a map from caller-chosen ids to typed question objects, and returning `{model, answers, usage}` with one typed answer per id ([API reference](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api)). The semantics that matter for an engine are stated plainly: "Jev ingests the `state` once and evaluates every question against it in parallel", "Questions in the same request are independent: one answer does not become context for another question", and "If a later judgment depends on an earlier answer, make a second request in code" ([Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models); [Primitives](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives)). The question id is never sent to the model, so ids can carry routing metadata; option names and descriptions are both sent, so descriptions must separate options from each other ([Choice](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/choice)).

| Primitive | Request `criteria` | Answer fields | Server-side `confidence` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `noul` | optional `{true, false}` descriptions | `noul` in [0,1], the probability of yes | none; TypeSafe suggests `abs(2p - 1)` |
| `choice` | map of up to 255 options to description or `null` | `choice`, `probabilities` (sum to 1), `confidence` | `(p_max - 1/n) / (1 - 1/n)` |
| `score` | ordered array of 2 to 10 level descriptions | `score` (probability-weighted level, can fall between levels), `legend`, `probabilities` (string keys), `confidence` | `max(0, 1 - spread/MAD_uniform)` around the modal level |
| `box` (Jev 1.14 only, experimental) | none | `box {top, bottom, left, right}`, `confidence` | undocumented; "always a box, there is no not-found answer" |

Sources for the table: [API reference](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api); [Confidence](https://docs.typesafe.ai/confidence); [Box](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/box). Two consequences follow. First, `confidence` can be recomputed client-side from `probabilities` with the published formulas, so an engine can normalise confidence across backends that return probabilities but no confidence field. Second, TypeSafe itself warns that `confidence` "describes the model's answer, not a guarantee that the answer is correct", that "Calibration is measured across groups of predictions; it does not guarantee that an individual answer is correct", and that two different distributions can produce the same Score ([Score](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/score); [System One](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/system-one)). Vals AI confirmed empirically that Jev's confidence "tracks its probability distribution almost exactly and adds no extra signal" ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/independent-evaluation-of-jev)). An engine should carry raw probabilities and derive its own notion of confidence.

Two versioned IDs exist, `jev-1.13.0` and `jev-1.14.0` (released 14 October 2026), plus the moving aliases `jev-latest` and `jev-preview`; TypeSafe says an alias "moves when a new release ships, so its answers can change without any change to your code", tells anyone who has "tuned confidence thresholds against one version" to pin the versioned ID, promises only 60 days' warning before deprecating 1.13.0 and 30 days for 1.14.0, and has not yet published a formal deprecation policy ([Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models); [Jev 1.14 blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/jev-1-14)). Default context is **64k tokens per request and 32k per question**, raisable to 262k with a `TypeSafe-Danger-Zone-Long-Context: true` header that TypeSafe describes as "slower and less accurate" ([Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models)). Pricing is $0.042 per million input tokens with output free and no premium for long context, and TypeSafe's own launch post concedes "We can't prove it isn't subsidized" ([Launch blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)). Batching is the core lever: TypeSafe's cookbook measured a 13-question regulatory briefing as **12.2x cheaper and 10.0x faster** batched than sequential, with no change in answers, because cost is dominated by state size rather than question count ([Parallel questions cookbook](https://docs.typesafe.ai/cookbooks/parallel_questions)). Rate limits are unpublished and per organisation; English is "where accuracy is currently best"; the same weights serve every account with no fine-tuning ([Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models)).

Latency claims and measurements diverge in an instructive way. TypeSafe quotes 70 to 500 ms end to end and "about 100 ms" typical, measured "from our laptops on the West Coast", and its own cookbook recorded a 111 ms mean ([Launch blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev); [Self-consistency cookbook](https://docs.typesafe.ai/cookbooks/consistency_noul_cookbook)). Independent figures range from Vals' 0.10 to 0.12 s for 1 to 64 judgments on one document, through OpenRouter's 175 ms median on Banking77, to Cloudflare's competitor-run **524 ms median and 536 ms p95**, and one Hermes plugin author measured TypeSafe direct at 1,431 ms against 732 ms through a gateway on identical answers ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/independent-evaluation-of-jev); [The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/workday-is-using-typesafes-jev-to-build-user-interfaces-on-the-fly/); [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/clef-decision-models); [Hermes docs](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/plugins/jev-skill-router)). An analysis of 12,759 launch tweets found a median user-reported speedup of 7x against the 193.6x headline, with median latency 76 ms and upper quartile 270 ms ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/typesafe-ai-jev-released/)). The practical reading is that a single decision costs 100 to 500 ms depending on route and region, and the headline speedups come from collapsing round trips, not from any one call.

### Official accuracy is 67.8% on TypeSafe's own suite; independent tests put it 3 to 7 points under frontier LLMs

TypeSafe's only published accuracy evidence is a self-run four-workflow benchmark whose reference labels are the averaged judgments of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1, and whose workflows were "built by the company's own team and may not reflect real-world use", as the launch post itself admits ([Launch blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)). On that suite Jev scores **67.8% aggregate against 74.1% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 73.1% for Claude Opus 5**, with the widest gap on invoice processing at 61.8% against 79.1% and the narrowest on customer service at 76.0% against 78.3%; the headline "444.6x cheaper" is Opus 5's $0.1761 per case divided by Jev's $0.0004, and "193.6x faster" is Sonnet 5's 78.1 s divided by Jev's 0.4 s, while against a Luna-class model the honest cost ratio is about 8x ([evals.typesafe.ai](https://evals.typesafe.ai/); [Projeckt AI](https://projecktai.substack.com/p/jev-claims-193x-faster-444x-cheaper)). The one official case study, Jack & Jill's candidate screening, replaced Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite on 100% of calls in one scoring stage and reports cost per 1,000 candidates falling from $0.755 to $0.092, median screening time from 20.3 s to 10.3 s, and winners kept in the shortlist rising from 93.9% to 94.6%, a difference the study itself calls not statistically significant (90% CI from -0.4 to +1.8 points) ([Jack & Jill case study](https://typesafe.ai/blog/jack-jill-jev-case-study)). That is the right template for an honest deployment claim: parity on accuracy, large wins on cost and latency.

Independent measurements, all at most a month old and most run by competitors or individuals, cluster into a clear pattern.

Where Jev matched or beat LLMs (LangSmith, MLflow, Near Here, Vals' claim verification, AG News), the task was a bounded classification over self-contained text with an answer set designed by the builder; where it trailed (invoice processing, LegalBench entailment, legal NER, poker, 77-way routing) the task needed numeric reasoning, multi-hop inference, or long-tail label semantics, exactly the nine "jaggedness" items TypeSafe lists for jev-1.13: literal reading, unreliable counting and arithmetic, dates read "as text, not as ordered quantities", indirection and double negatives, accuracy that "falls as the state grows with content unrelated to the decision", adversarial content that "can move the answer", contradictory instructions, a lean "toward the option that comes first", and no generation ([Jev 1.13 jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13)). Competitor-run leaderboards sharpen the picture: on Cloudflare's numbers Jev loses Banking77 to Clef 79.7 to 94.2 but wins the knowledge-heavy sets, GPQA Diamond 78.3 to 48.0 and MMLU-Pro 82.7 to 65.9, and Perplexity's 27B open decider edges it 85.71 to 84.51 overall across eleven benchmarks while Jev wins six of them ([Hugging Face Cloudflare/clef](https://huggingface.co/Cloudflare/clef); [Hugging Face pplx-decider-v1-27b](https://huggingface.co/perplexity-ai/pplx-decider-v1-27b)). The "zero hallucinations" claim is true only of output type; TypeSafe says the schema-matching rate "is set to 0% by construction, not measured", and the Hacker News launch thread's main pushback was that "if it puts a high confidence value on a wrong answer, thats still hallucinating" ([Launch blog](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev); [HN item 49717558](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49717558)).

### Calibration ranks errors well but shifts with the base rate; margin, order and injection are the live threats

TypeSafe publishes no reliability diagram, ECE or Brier score for any Jev version; a pentest-harness paper's claim that "TypeSafe reports ECE of 0.246" could not be traced to any TypeSafe page ([arXiv 2609.28940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28940)). Four independent groups have measured calibration and the spread is the finding. Vals AI's preregistered twelve-system study found Jev's **ECE was 0.011 on claim verification, the lowest of twelve, but 0.108 on LegalBench, the highest**, concluding that "calibration on one benchmark did not transfer to the other"; at a 1% error budget with thresholds set on 30% of data, Jev automated 95% of claim cases at a realised 1.6% error, over budget ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/independent-evaluation-of-jev)). The "Just Ask Jev" study over 44 benchmarks found a pooled reliability curve close to the diagonal (ECE 0.047) but a **per-benchmark median ECE of 0.168**, with 24 of 31 benchmarks exceeding the perfect-calibration null, attributed to base-rate mismatch (median gap of 0.125 between mean probability and positive rate) rather than ranking (median AUROC 0.905); the **median F1-optimal threshold was 0.35, not 0.5**, and fitting the threshold on only ten labelled items raised median F1 from 0.706 to 0.793 ([arXiv 2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29429)). On legal span extraction Jev was overconfident with ECE 0.17 to 0.44; on a 150-passage test its ECE was 0.121 with 34.7% of outputs in the 0.35 to 0.65 band; one routing test logged 17 confident errors at probability 0.90 or above ([jevextract](https://github.com/gabazureus/jevextract); [infinisynapse](https://infinisynapse.com/en/blog/jev-benchmark)). The honest summary is that Jev's probabilities rank well but are not absolutely calibrated per task, which is precisely the condition under which a per-task threshold fit on a few dozen labelled items is both necessary and cheap.

Run-to-run stability is a different property and is genuinely strong: TypeSafe's own 14-Noul by 15-run test gave a probability standard deviation of 0.0102, LangSmith's 500-decision runs put Jev's variance 92 to 913 times below LLM judges, and Vals saw identical answers on all five repeats of 25 items ([Self-consistency cookbook](https://docs.typesafe.ai/cookbooks/consistency_noul_cookbook); [LangChain](https://langchain.com/blog/jev-agent-evals-langsmith); [Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/independent-evaluation-of-jev)). LangChain's own caveat, "low variance does not mean high accuracy", is borne out by a ContractNLI study where five of Jev's consistently answered anchors were "stable wrong", and by Vals finding that on `contract_nli_permissible_copy` Jev answered Yes to all 33 items against a gold split of 16 to 17 ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/independent-evaluation-of-jev)). Near-determinism has a design consequence the math section makes precise: re-asking the same question of the same model adds almost no information, so self-consistency ensembles cannot help, and the only redundancy that pays is diversity in framing, decomposition, or model.

Three threats operate on the decision margin. First, option order: TypeSafe admits a first-option lean; one hosted test reversing options shifted probabilities from 0.84 to 0.89 up to 0.93 to 0.96 and dropped correct classifications from 16 of 16 to 12 of 16, while another found hosted jev-1.13.0 changed 0 of 6 labels across three orderings but moved confidence by 0.13 to 0.16 against at most 0.04 between identical repeats ([infinisynapse](https://infinisynapse.com/en/blog/jev-benchmark); [jev-position-test](https://github.com/imaddde867/jev-position-test)). Second, adversarial evidence: Check Point's agentic attackers broke a Jev due-diligence assistant in 25 of 27 configurations at about $0.50 per break, succeeding by appending a plausible audit addendum containing "no instructions to the model", and **typed input and an "untrusted" marker made no meaningful difference** (16, 16 and 13 breaks of 27) while explicit anti-injection instructions moved breaks from 18 to 17; JevOut found short, natural, non-instructional context redirected 61.4 to 73.2% of initially correct decisions to a chosen wrong option across four decision systems including Jev, often with high confidence ([Check Point](https://blog.checkpoint.com/ai-security/jev-is-not-a-language-model-but-it-breaks-like-one-prompt-injection-against-a-typed-decision-model/); [arXiv 2609.30243](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30243)). A fake "pre-approved" field moved P(block) on `rm -rf ~/.ssh` from 0.76 to 0.48 ([Projeckt AI](https://projecktai.substack.com/p/jev-claims-193x-faster-444x-cheaper)). Third, and unifying the first two, the Decision Hijacking study found that over 510 reconstructed InjecAgent cases the base attack succeeded only 1.8% of the time overall, but **12 of 12 times when the decision margin was at most 0.1 and 1 of 468 times otherwise** ([arXiv 2609.28613](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.28613)). Decisions within about a tenth of the threshold are the ones that flip; the engine's review band should be defined by margin, not by a single cutoff.

### Measured deployments win on latency and cost, tie on bounded classification, and fail in agent loops nobody shadowed

An ecosystem survey counted 2,170 public GitHub projects using Jev by 22 September, concentrated in attribute judgment and scoring, with public attention concentrating in routing and interface agents ([arXiv 2609.30216](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30216)). The deployments that published numbers share one shape: code enumerates a bounded option set or a battery of yes/no checks, Jev answers them all in one call, code applies thresholds and escalates the rest. Browser Use's `jev-ultrafast` picks one of eight operations plus a target element from an indexed element table, asking the operation and all target heads speculatively in one request and executing only the matching head; over six alternating runs against a screenshot baseline both passed 3 of 3 while median task time fell from 9.450 to 7.092 s and **browser-protocol calls fell from 1,092 to 101**, and the README states the benchmark "is not a general reliability benchmark" ([jev-ultrafast](https://github.com/browser-use/jev-ultrafast)). Workday's Sana builds its home-screen UI by having Jev choose among every atomic component in the design system with "none of the selection logic" hard-coded, but disclosed neither the component count nor what happens when nothing fits ([The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/workday-is-using-typesafes-jev-to-build-user-interfaces-on-the-fly/)). Vercel's claim that Jev made its command-safety classifier "about 5 to 18 times faster" and "more accurate" has no published dataset, p95 or accuracy delta ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/18/a-new-kind-of-ai-model-from-a-chatgpt-inventor-is-thrilling-developers/)).

The strongest measured levers sit upstream of the model. Pre-digesting state in code raised a 70-scenario NPC decision task from **74.3% to 100%** by banding `hp: 38, maxHp: 250` into "critical", converting timestamps to relative labels and pruning fields, while cutting input tokens 43% ([jev-state](https://github.com/suranjaychandra/jev-state)). DSPy's ReAnchor, which fits thresholds, Score cuts and Choice weights against an end-to-end metric with a five-fold acceptance check, lifted email triage from 0.692 to 0.749 on 1,000 unseen-sender test emails and a report judge from 0.813 to 0.874, touching no instructions ([DSPy PR #10475](https://github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy/pull/10475)). Fusing Jev's rerank with an embedding retriever by reciprocal rank fusion added **+0.090 NDCG@10 (CI +0.077 to +0.104)** where Jev alone added a non-significant +0.012, and the same study quantified judge circularity: the Jev-versus-baseline comparison read +0.053 under Jev-only labels and -0.028 under LLM-only labels ([jev-search-rerank-eval](https://github.com/zhuyansen/jev-search-rerank-eval)). By contrast, cascading to a bigger model buys little: Rao and Callison-Burch found a decision-model-first stage with an LLM fallback gained at most 2.7 points over the strongest single judge even with oracle thresholds, "because the fallbacks fail where the first stage fails", with **more than 85% of flash-tier verdicts on Jev's most confident errors repeating those errors** where independent errors would give about 50% ([arXiv 2609.29769](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29769)). Selective prediction, however, works as the theory predicts: keeping the half of decisions with the largest margin raised median accuracy from 0.793 to 0.933 ([arXiv 2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29429)).

Two cautionary results matter more than any success. Thresholding each sub-question of a decomposed rubric at 0.5 lost to the best single targeted question on 9 of 10 benchmarks, a median -0.137 AUROC, while a three-level Score read as an expected level beat a generic Noul on 19 of 31 and argmax readouts "lose almost everywhere" ([arXiv 2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29429)). Decomposition is TypeSafe's "most important concept", but recombining thresholded booleans with AND is the wrong reduction; combine in probability or log-odds space and fit the threshold on the composite. And the one published week-long shadow run ended in removal: a skill router in Claude Code produced 1,242 judgments and 539 skill suggestions of which **only 28, about 5%, were used within 30 minutes**, 13 of 20 reviewed unused picks were off target, and the author's conclusion was that "a judgment model is easy to evaluate in a fixed pipeline" but in agent loops "adding a component can change downstream behavior in hard-to-see ways" ([dev.to](https://dev.to/shimo4228/is-there-any-point-to-this-removing-the-jev-plugins-i-added-to-claude-code-after-one-week-49eh)). Guardrail plugins report precision but not recall: pi-warden held 42 of 17,000 commands with about 88% of holds judged correct and false negatives unreported, and every guard author (jevkit, jev-shield, hermes-jev-approvals, Pydantic AI) states that Jev is "a reviewer, not a sandbox" ([pi-warden write-up](https://viblo.asia/p/dung-jev-lam-guardrail-cho-coding-agent-case-study-pi-warden-voi-17000-luot-phan-doan-that-RlL5eP0GLbB); [Pydantic AI](https://pydantic.dev/docs/ai/models/typesafe/)).

Two contractual facts constrain the engine. The Master Customer Agreement forbids making the Services available "as a standalone service" and using any Output "to perform model distillation, train a model to imitate the output of the Services, or develop ... a similar or competing product or service", which sits in tension with training a downstream classifier on Jev probabilities and needs legal review; it also states in capitals that "THE SERVICES MAY PRODUCE INACCURATE OR ERRONEOUS OUTPUT" and that the customer "IS RESPONSIBLE FOR INDEPENDENTLY EVALUATING THE OUTPUT", caps liability at the greater of twelve months' fees or $50, grants TypeSafe telemetry rights "in perpetuity", and offers only "commercially reasonable efforts" at notice of breaking API changes ([MCA](https://typesafe.ai/legal/mca)). The Acceptable Use Policy contains no high-risk-use clause for employment, credit or medical decisions, unlike Microsoft's Decision-1 card, which reportedly advises against sole-basis consequential decisions about people ([AUP](https://typesafe.ai/legal/acceptable-use-policy); [Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/introducing-microsoft-decision-1-fast-ai-for-real-time-decisions-e6344144ad25)). The absence of a vendor restriction is not permission; the human-factors section explains why.

## Independent 80% votes hit 99.9% in eight calls; correlated ones stall near 97%

### Parallel aggregation and the correlation ceiling

Condorcet's jury theorem is the exact statement of when 80% compounds: with independent voters each correct with probability p above one half, majority error decays exponentially in n, and the proof is a two-line comparison showing that adding two voters flips a wrong majority to right more often than the reverse if and only if p exceeds a half ([Condorcet's jury theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet%27s_jury_theorem)). The numbers at p = 0.8, computed exactly from the binomial formula, are the first table every engineer should internalise, and the second table shows what pairwise error correlation does to them under the beta-binomial exchangeable model in which the asymptotic jury accuracy is P(theta > 1/2), a strict ceiling below one for any positive correlation.

| n voters | independent, rho = 0 | rho = 0.05 | rho = 0.10 | rho = 0.20 | rho = 0.30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 0.896 | 0.883 | 0.871 | 0.849 | 0.814 |
| 9 | 0.980 | 0.955 | 0.930 | 0.888 | 0.839 |
| 15 | 0.996 | 0.974 | 0.947 | 0.899 | 0.846 |
| 51 | 0.9999992 | 0.992 | 0.967 | 0.912 | 0.853 |
| ceiling as n grows | 1 | 0.9973 | 0.9755 | 0.9201 | 0.8753 |

Independent 80% voters reach 95% at 7, 99% at 13, **99.9% at 21** and 99.99% at 31; the error exponent is roughly 2n(p - 1/2)^2, so the gain is brutally sensitive to the edge, and at p = 0.55 about 1,001 voters are needed for 99.9%. Correlation does not shift the curve; it flattens it. At rho = 0.2 a 51-voter jury (0.912) barely beats 9 voters (0.888) and never beats 0.92. The theoretical basis is Ladha's result that the asymptotic half of the theorem survives only if average correlation is bounded, and Pivato's that convergence requires average correlation to shrink as the population grows ([Ladha 1992](https://doi.org/10.2307/2111584); [Pivato, MPRA 69546](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/69546/)). The 2026 measurements on LLM judge panels are this table observed in the wild: a nine-frontier-LLM panel across seven model families delivered **about two independent votes' worth** of information, falling 8 to 22 points short of what independent voting would achieve, with the best single judge matching or beating the full panel in every condition; a 32-judge panel had a human-equivalent size of 2.3 to 6.5 with an extrapolated asymptote below four ([arXiv 2605.29800](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29800); [arXiv 2609.21277](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21277)). The engineering quantity is the Kish effective sample size n_eff = n / (1 + (n - 1) rho_bar), measured from logs as pairwise error correlation on items with known answers; use n_eff, not n, in the independent table.

When competences differ and are known, the optimal rule is weighted majority with log-odds weights w_i = ln(p_i / (1 - p_i)), which is naive Bayes (Nitzan and Paroush, as presented in [Berend and Kontorovich](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.0451)); three voters at (0.9, 0.7, 0.6) give 0.834 by simple majority, worse than the best voter alone, and exactly 0.900 weighted, and adding two 0.6 voters to three 0.8 voters drops a simple majority from 0.896 to 0.869 while a weighted vote stays at 0.896. Unweighted voting with weak members hurts; log-odds weighting makes them harmless. When competences are unknown, Dawid and Skene's EM estimates each labeller's confusion matrix from agreement patterns alone, and a 2026 label-free variant for LLM judges tracked an oracle that knew true error rates to within 0.5 points ([Dawid and Skene 1979](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2346806); [arXiv 2609.12002](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12002)). Boosting is the formal proof that weak learners compound into strong ones, with training error bounded by exp(-2 sum gamma_t^2), but its mechanism is reweighting toward the cases prior learners got wrong, which plain voting lacks ([Schapire, Explaining AdaBoost](https://rob.schapire.net/papers/explaining-adaboost.pdf)).

Von Neumann adds the constraint that the aggregator itself is a noisy component. Reliable computation from unreliable parts is possible only below a per-gate failure threshold, about 0.0073 in his original majority-organ construction and exactly **1/6 for three-input majority gates** (Hajek and Weller), with an iterated restoring organ whose gate noise d settles at a floor of order d and merges with 1/2 at d = 1/6 ([Probabilistic Logics](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/Probabilistic_Logics.pdf); [Hajek-Weller 1991](https://hajek.ece.illinois.edu/Papers/HajekWeller91.pdf); [Unger](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0711.0351)). An 80% oracle is above threshold: it can be a voter (p > 1/2 is all Condorcet needs) but it cannot be the vote-counter, the verifier or the comparer in its own compounding scheme; those must be deterministic code. Shannon frames the same point as efficiency: a binary symmetric channel with 20% crossover has capacity 1 - H(0.2) = 0.278 bits per use, so a single calibrated 0.8 call carries a log-likelihood ratio of ln 4, exactly two bits of evidence, but only 0.278 bits of expected information, and majority voting is the repetition code whose rate goes to zero as error goes to zero; structured use of calls, with each answering a different sub-question and deterministic checks between, is the "better code" ([Binary symmetric channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_symmetric_channel); [Noisy-channel coding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noisy-channel_coding_theorem)).

### Sequential chains decay as 0.8^n unless a verifier's catch rate intervenes

Sequential evidence accumulation is cheaper than fixed juries. Because a calibrated 0.8 call contributes exactly 1.386 nats, Bayesian updating is counting net votes and the posterior after a lead of d is 1 / (1 + 0.25^d); Wald's SPRT turns this into a stopping rule that minimises expected samples at given error rates, and the "first to lead by k" rule has success probability 1 / (1 + ((1-p)/p)^k), a gambler's-ruin result ([SPRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_probability_ratio_test); [MAKER, arXiv 2511.09030](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09030v1)). At p = 0.8, lead-by-3 gives 98.5% in 4.9 expected calls, **lead-by-4 gives 99.6% in 6.6 calls, lead-by-5 gives 99.9% in 8.3 calls**, against 21 calls for a fixed majority at 99.9%, a roughly 2.5x saving; MAKER proves that the minimum k for a target success over s steps grows as Theta(ln s), so total cost is Theta(s ln s) and wall-clock stays linear because votes are parallel ([MAKER](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09030v1)). MAKER is also the existence proof: a 20-disk Towers of Hanoi of 1,048,575 moves completed with zero errors using gpt-4.1-mini at a measured per-step error of 0.0022, lead-by-3 voting, and "red-flagging" that discards and resamples overly long or malformed outputs. At p = 0.8 the same scheme needs k = 8 for a 1,000-step chain at 95% end-to-end, about 13,300 calls for 1,000 decisions; usable, but four to five times more expensive per decision than a 0.998 oracle, and only if errors are independent.

The unchecked chain is where the thesis inverts.

| steps n | 0.8^n | 0.9^n | 0.99^n | 0.999^n |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 0.512 | 0.729 | 0.970 | 0.997 |
| 10 | 0.107 | 0.349 | 0.904 | 0.990 |
| 20 | 0.0115 | 0.122 | 0.818 | 0.980 |
| 100 | 2.0e-10 | 2.7e-5 | 0.366 | 0.905 |
| 50% horizon H = ln 0.5 / ln p | 3.1 steps | 6.6 | 69 | 693 |

The horizon formula H_s(p) = ln s / ln p is from Sinha et al., who also showed that "a small improvement in step accuracy can lead to a large increase in horizon length" once accuracy is high, and that per-step accuracy degrades as a model's own prior errors accumulate in its context ("self-conditioning"), which model scale does not remove and chain-of-thought does not mitigate ([arXiv 2509.09677](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09677v3)). METR measures the 50% time horizon doubling every roughly seven months with the **80% horizon four to six times shorter**, and the constant-p model predicts only a 3.1x ratio, which says real per-step reliability is not constant along a task ([arXiv 2503.14499](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v3)). A 2026 study across nine models found agentic task success "follows a geometric law governed by a single per-step reliability parameter" that "saturates well below 1 even for the strongest models", every model falling "from near-perfect success to near zero within sixteen steps" on the tool-use task, and that bounding the context window made decay steeper, not gentler ([arXiv 2609.01660](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01660)). tau-bench's pass^k metric, the probability of succeeding on all of k runs, is the measurement that separates coverage from reliability: state-of-the-art agents had pass^8 below 25% in retail against pass^1 around 61% ([arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)).

The chain is broken by a verifier after each step with resampling on rejection. If a step is correct with probability p, the verifier catches wrong outputs with probability t and wrongly rejects correct ones with probability f, then the accepted output is correct with probability q = p(1-f) / [p(1-f) + (1-p)(1-t)], expected attempts are 1 / [p(1-f) + (1-p)(1-t)], and n-step success is q^n.

| verifier (t catch, f false alarm) at p = 0.8 | per-step q | attempts | 10-step | 100-step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| none | 0.800 | 1.00 | 0.107 | 2e-10 |
| another 80% oracle (0.80, 0.20) | 0.9412 | 1.47 | 0.545 | 0.0023 |
| (0.95, 0.05) | 0.9870 | 1.30 | 0.878 | 0.271 |
| (0.99, 0.01) | 0.9975 | 1.26 | 0.975 | 0.777 |
| strict (0.95, 0.20) | 0.9846 | 1.54 | 0.856 | 0.212 |
| deterministic partial check (0.50, 0.00) | 0.8889 | 1.11 | 0.308 | about 0 |

Three readings. The catch rate t sets reliability; the false-alarm rate f only costs retries (f of 0.20 against 0.05 at t = 0.95 moves q from 0.987 to 0.985 but attempts from 1.30 to 1.54), so prefer strict verifiers. An 80% oracle verifying an 80% oracle yields 0.941 per step, identical to requiring two agreeing votes, and is nowhere near enough for long chains. Deterministic checks (tests, schema validation, re-execution) have f near zero and stack as t_total = 1 - prod(1 - t_i). The empirical literature agrees with the arithmetic: intrinsic self-correction without external signal does not work and can degrade performance, poor self-correction stems from an inability to find mistakes rather than fix them, LLM evaluators recognise and prefer their own outputs with a linear link between self-recognition and self-preference, and Anthropic's harness team found "tuning a standalone evaluator to be skeptical was found to be far more tractable than making a generator critical of its own work" ([arXiv 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798); [arXiv 2404.13076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076v1); [Anthropic harness design](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). Where verifiers are independent and cheap they pay: a 3B web-navigation process reward model beat GPT-4o as a step judge by about 30 points and added 10.9 points to a GPT-4o-mini policy at about one-tenth the verifier cost, while GPT-4o-mini verifying itself added 0.6 ([Web-Shepherd](https://huggingface.co/papers/2505.15277)). Where verifiers are gamed they fail: a 6B verifier's best-of-N accuracy fell past 400 samples because the generator found "adversarial solutions that fool the verifier", and coverage on MATH rose from 82.9% to 98.44% across 100 to 10,000 samples while majority voting and reward models moved only from 40.5% to 41.4% ([arXiv 2110.14168](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168); [arXiv 2407.21787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787)).

Ratcheting, locking in each verified step so a later failure costs only a local retry, converts exponential cost into linear cost: for q = 0.9412 over 100 steps, an all-or-nothing chain expects 1/q^100 = 430 restarts while ratcheting every step expects about 106 step-attempts. But a ratchet locks in whatever passed, including accepted-wrong outputs at rate 1 - q, so after n ratcheted steps the probability of no latent error is still q^n. Ratcheting fixes cost, not correctness; the verifier fixes correctness. Two further rules follow from the evidence. Retries must be fresh draws: Sinha's self-conditioning result means a retry in the same context with the error visible is not independent, and MAKER's red-flagging discards the bad output before resampling in a clean microagent context ([arXiv 2509.09677](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09677v3); [MAKER](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09030v1)). And verification budget should concentrate on irreversible steps: SABER found each deviation in a state-mutating action reduced the odds of task success "by up to 92% on Airline and up to 96% on Retail" while non-mutating deviations had "little to no effect" ([arXiv 2512.07850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07850)).

### Cascades, abstention and thresholds work only on calibrated probabilities

Chow's 1970 rule says the optimal reject rule under symmetric costs is a threshold on the maximum posterior, and Geifman and El-Yaniv's selective classification makes it operational with a high-probability bound: a 2% top-5 error on ImageNet guaranteed with 99.9% probability at nearly 60% coverage ([Chow 1970](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1970.1054406); [arXiv 1705.08500](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500)). Cascades monetise the reject option: FrugalGPT's learned three-model cascade with a DistilBERT reliability scorer and per-stage thresholds matched GPT-4 on HEADLINES at a 98.3% cost saving, and 2026 work replaces raw thresholds with conformal prediction set size, accepting only when the calibrated set "collapses to a single answer" with a union-bound coverage guarantee of at least 1 - K alpha across K tiers ([arXiv 2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176); [arXiv 2607.25018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25018)). Split conformal prediction is the distribution-free replacement for "trust the 0.8": with n exchangeable calibration points the prediction set's coverage lies in [1 - alpha, 1 - alpha + 1/(n+1)] with no model assumptions, so 1,000 calibration points at alpha = 0.1 give coverage between 0.900 and 0.901, marginally over items rather than per item ([Angelopoulos and Bates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511)). "Trust or Escalate" showed a 7B judge can guarantee over 80% human agreement at almost 80% coverage on a Chatbot Arena subset where GPT-4 alone almost never reached 80% ([arXiv 2407.18370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18370)).

The toy arithmetic shows why calibration is load-bearing. A cheap oracle whose calibrated confidences are 0.6 on 30% of items, 0.8 on 30% and 0.95 on 40% (80% overall) with a 98%-accurate strong oracle at ten times the cost gives accuracy 0.80 at cost 1 escalating nothing, 0.914 at cost 4 escalating below 0.7, 0.968 at cost 7 escalating below 0.9, and 0.98 at cost 11 escalating everything, which is worse than calling the strong oracle alone. The optimal threshold is a Lagrangian: escalate bucket b iff (a_strong - c_b) times the value of a correct answer exceeds the strong oracle's cost. If the "0.95" bucket is really 0.85, the cascade silently under-escalates. A perfectly calibrated oracle that always says 0.8 and is right 80% of the time has Brier score 0.16 with zero reliability error and zero resolution, and is useless for selective prediction because there is no uncertain subset to escalate; Murphy's decomposition, Brier = Reliability - Resolution + Uncertainty, is therefore the pair of numbers to log, since cascades need calibration plus resolution ([Brier score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brier_score)). Calibration itself is measurable from outcome logs by binning (ECE with 15 bins, as in Guo et al.) and fixable by a one-parameter temperature that changes no argmax decision, which took ResNet-110's ECE on CIFAR-100 from 16.5% to 1.26% ([Guo et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1706.04599)). A reliability bin's accuracy has a 95% half-width of 1.96 sqrt(c(1-c)/n), so **about 250 to 400 outcomes per bin pin accuracy within five points**; treat any "0.8" as unverified below that. Feeding overconfident 0.95s into a Bayesian update when the true hit rate is 0.8 inflates the odds by 4.75x per call; after four such calls the engine believes 99.99% when the truth is 99.6%, and an SPRT stops early with higher real error than nominal.

Decision theory supplies the thresholds themselves. With a calibrated probability q of being right and costs C_FP for acting wrongly and C_FN for failing to act, act iff q exceeds C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN) ([Elkan 2001](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~elkan/rescale.pdf)). A single 0.8 call justifies acting only when a false positive costs less than four times a false negative; for a 10:1 asymmetry the posterior must exceed 0.909, which needs a net lead of two independent votes, and 100:1 needs 0.990, a lead of four. The value of one more 80% vote when the prior is 0.8 is exactly zero if the engine must act either way, because at posterior 0.5 it still acts on the tie-break and at 0.941 it acts the same; it becomes 0.128 per vote only when disagreement can trigger an abstain or escalate branch costing 0.1 ([Value of information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_of_information)). This is the decision-theoretic reason an engine without an abstention path cannot benefit from redundancy, and why SPRT has two boundaries. Buying cheap votes beats escalating to a 98% strong oracle at 10x price for every cost of error if the votes are independent (lead-by-4 costs 6.6 calls and leaves 0.39% error against 10 calls and 2% error), and the thing that reverses it is not price but correlation: under rho = 0.1 the cheap oracle's ceiling is 0.9755, so no number of cheap votes reaches 0.98 and escalation becomes the only route below 2.4% error. The pivotal input to buy-versus-escalate is the measured correlation ceiling, and the measured fact that LLM fallbacks repeat Jev's confident errors more than 85% of the time says the "escalate to a frontier model" branch is itself a correlated vote, not an independent one ([arXiv 2609.29769](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29769)).

Kelly closes the loop between calibration and survival. At p = 0.8 and even odds the growth-optimal stake is 60% of bankroll for 19.3% log-growth per bet; staking 95% shrinks the bankroll almost surely and 100% is ruin on the first loss ([Kelly criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_criterion)). If the oracle says 0.8 but is really 0.6, the correct stake is 0.2, and staking 0.6 gives growth of -0.085 per bet: a bankroll that shrinks almost surely while every individual bet looks positive in expectation. Overconfidence is the overbetting failure mode, fractional Kelly is the hedge against residual miscalibration, and "exposure per automated decision" is the engineering translation.

## The literature says calibration compounds; correctness does not

### Fast judgment is trustworthy only in kind environments with closed feedback

Cognitive science does not say fast judgment is bad; it says when it can be trusted. Kahneman and Klein's adversarial collaboration concluded that whether an intuition can be trusted depends on "assessing how predictable the environment is and whether the person has had a real chance to learn its regularities", and that "subjective confidence is not a reliable sign of accuracy" ([Kahneman and Klein 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755)). Hogarth's distinction between kind learning environments, where feedback is accurate and plentiful, and wicked ones, where it is missing, delayed or misleading, formalises the same point ([Hogarth, Lejarraga and Soyer 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721415591878)). Evans and Stanovich define Type 1 processing by autonomy, running when triggering conditions are met without working memory, with speed only a correlate ([Evans and Stanovich 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612460685)); "System One model" is a good name under that definition and the gating irony resolves cleanly, because in the human case System 2 sets the policy and System 1 executes, so thresholds and escalation rules are the software analogue of oversight and the confidence field is the "feeling of knowing" that must be validated against outcomes. The Kahneman-Klein criteria become a per-task admission test for automation: a stable cue-to-outcome relationship, an outcome observable quickly and unambiguously, and demonstrated competence on that specific regularity, since expertise fractionates and so does machine competence, as Jev's 0.011 versus 0.108 ECE on two legal-adjacent tasks shows.

Simon licenses "good enough" as rational given bounded computation, and Gigerenzer's school licenses it as sometimes more accurate: in uncertain, data-sparse environments simple cue-limited heuristics can beat information-greedy models by trading bias for variance, and "ignoring information can improve accuracy" ([Simon 1956](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042769); [Gigerenzer and Brighton 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2008.01006.x)). The less-is-more claim is contested on the grounds that it compares heuristics to poorly regularised full models ([Jason Collins](https://www.jasoncollins.blog/posts/in-contrast-to-less-is-more-claims-ignoring-information-is-rarely-if-ever-optimal)), but both camps agree that the heuristic must be matched to the environment, an empirical fact about the world rather than a property of the heuristic. For a typed decision model this argues for small, curated, versioned cue schemas per decision type and empirical pruning of fields that do not improve out-of-sample Brier, which is exactly what the jev-state experiment and TypeSafe's "filter first; send only what the question needs" guidance found. Neither Simon nor Gigerenzer claims that many good-enough decisions compound into excellence; both are claims about single decisions.

### Decision quality, forecasting and epistemic democracy hinge on independence and calibration

The Stanford decision-analysis tradition supplies the strongest single argument for the thesis. Howard distinguishes a good decision, logically consistent with the alternatives, information and preferences at the time, from a good outcome, and argues decisions should be judged only on the former because outcomes are partly chance ([Howard 1988](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.34.6.679)). A calibrated 80%-confident decision that turns out wrong was a good decision; this only works if the probability was truthfully calibrated. Duke names "resulting", judging a decision by its outcome, as the central error, and Parrish's decision journal records "assigned probabilities, including confidence level" before the outcome and reviews later to fight hindsight bias ([Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)). A decision engine that logs (type, cues, probability, action, outcome, time-to-outcome) is a decision journal at machine scale, and Brier decomposition per type is the review step.

Forecasting research shows aggregation works but only under conditions. The Good Judgment Project's aggregation beat the simple average by more than 60% in Brier terms by weighting better forecasters and "extremizing", and whether to extremize "depends on how diverse and sophisticated the pool of forecasters is"; Satopää, Pemantle and Ungar showed the right amount of extremizing rises with information diversity and falls to zero when forecasters share the same information ([AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-judgment-project/); [Satopää et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.2148v2)). Calling one model N times on the same input is N forecasters with near-total information overlap, so justified extremizing is near zero and effective crowd size near one. Tetlock's finding that tracking, feedback on Brier scores, was one of only three interventions that improved human forecasters is direct support for the learning-loop reading of compounding ([Mellers et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614524255)). The mirror image is Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch's informational cascade: when individuals decide sequentially and observe predecessors' actions but not their signals, it becomes rational after a few decisions to copy, and the crowd converges on a possibly wrong answer with very little aggregate information ([Bikhchandani et al. 1992](https://doi.org/10.1086/261849)). A routing decision feeding a classification feeding an action, each passing only its label forward, is a cascade with the model's shared bias as the first mover.

Epistemic democracy turns on the same conditions. Condorcet's theorem requires independence, binary choice and competence above a half, and Ladha showed correlated errors can make a larger jury less accurate than a smaller one ([Ladha 1992](https://doi.org/10.2307/2111584)). The popular "diversity trumps ability" theorem of Hong and Page is, in Thompson's critique, "little more than a straightforward restatement of its hypotheses", with the accompanying simulation showing mainly that random algorithms can work well, and the measured reply is that "diversity trumps ability in some cases, ability trumps diversity in others" ([Hong and Page 2004](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0403723101); [Thompson 2014](https://www.ams.org/notices/201409/rnoti-p1024.pdf); [Grim et al. 2019](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/philosophy-of-science/article/diversity-ability-and-expertise-in-epistemic-communities/E679089194FE0FB848F597F3CD1F8A70)). The honest version is Page's identity that collective squared error equals average individual error minus prediction diversity, which for an engine is a design metric: measure prediction diversity across the ensemble, and if it is near zero the ensemble is one judge. Estlund's epistemic proceduralism adds that being right does not confer authority to decide ("the expert/boss fallacy"); authority comes from the procedure, which anticipates the accountability findings below ([SEP: Democracy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/democracy/)).

### Reversibility, ruin and requisite variety are the non-negotiable constraints

The practitioners who talk about compounding mean two different things. Hamming's "knowledge and productivity are like compound interest" is about reinvesting a stock of learning ([Hamming 1986](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html)); Munger's "consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent" is about avoiding error long enough for averages to apply ([Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2025/08/25/not-stupid/)). The "1% better every day" arithmetic (1.01^365 is about 37.8) is pseudo-math because it assumes multiplicative, independent, non-decaying gains on a stock that reinvests; the same arithmetic gives 0.99^365 of about 0.026, and uncorrected errors persist. Bezos's framework is the most directly usable: "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had", but explicitly for "two-way doors" that "can use a light-weight process", while "consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible" one-way doors "must be made methodically, carefully, slowly", and the organisational failure is applying the heavy process to everything ([Bezos 2016 letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders); [Farnam Street on the 2015 letter](https://fs.blog/reversible-irreversible-decisions/)). The two-way-door test is a property of a decision type that can be declared in a schema. Taleb and Kelly supply the hard constraint: at millions of decisions any per-decision probability of ruin q, however small, makes ruin certain since (1 - q)^n goes to zero, and Peters' ergodicity argument is that time averages and expectation values diverge for growth processes, so expected-value reasoning misdescribes what one system experiences over time ([Peters 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-019-0732-0); [Kelly 1956](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1956.tb03809.x)). The only robust fix is to make ruin impossible per decision by capping blast radius and never auto-executing one-way doors, not to make it improbable. Dalio's "believability-weighted" decisions, trust indexed to track record on that kind of question, are the human precedent for per-type thresholds and match Kahneman-Klein's fractionation of expertise.

Cybernetics supplies the mechanism. Ashby's law of requisite variety, "only variety can destroy variety", says a regulator can hold outcomes within bounds only if the variety of its responses matches the variety of disturbances; a typed model with k output values has variety k and the world has more, so the escalation path to a slower model or a human is where the missing variety lives, and removing it to save cost makes the regulator inadequate by theorem ([Ashby, Introduction to Cybernetics](http://pespmc1.vub.ac.be/books/IntroCyb.pdf); [Variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_(cybernetics))). Conant and Ashby's good regulator theorem, "every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system", makes distribution shift the central threat rather than an edge case ([Conant and Ashby 1970](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207727008920220)). Beer's Viable System Model gives the organisational checklist: operations (System 1) need coordination so parallel decisions do not oscillate (System 2), sporadic audit independent of operations (System 3*), which is the only way to catch errors the engine is confident about, and environmental intelligence (System 4); most decision-engine designs ship System 1 alone ([Viable system model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viable_system_model)). Popper's piecemeal engineering is the philosophical warrant for many small reversible decisions over a few big ones: not because small decisions are more often right but because their failures are isolable and survivable, conditional on each decision carrying a falsifiable prediction and on the loop actually closing ([SEP: Karl Popper](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/)). Peirce's truth as "the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate" reframes the right label for a decision type as the limit of outcome feedback over time, so thresholds should stay fallible and never freeze ([SEP: Peirce](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce/)). Aristotle's habituation, "we become just by doing just acts", supports learning thresholds from outcomes if correction is present, but phronesis includes perceiving what is salient and grasping the ends worth pursuing, which a fixed cue schema cannot do; the engine may habituate its means while accountable humans set and re-justify the ends, with novelty detection as an escalation trigger independent of confidence ([SEP: Aristotle's Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)).

### Human factors: the 20% escalation queue is Bainbridge's irony in its purest form

The standard architecture, automate the easy 80% and send the hard 20% to a human, is a known trap. Bainbridge's ironies of automation hold that the operator's job becomes monitoring, which humans do poorly over long periods, and that the skills needed to take over decay precisely because the automation usually works, so the operator is least prepared when most needed ([Bainbridge 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8)). Parasuraman and Manzey found automation complacency and automation bias occur "in both naive and expert participants" and cannot be overcome with simple practice, training or instructions ([Parasuraman and Manzey 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055)). Elish's "moral crumple zone" predicts the nearest human absorbs the blame "no matter how little or unintentionally they are involved", as blame for the 2018 Uber fatality moved from the company to the safety driver within days ([Elish 2019](https://doi.org/10.17351/ests2019.260)). The escalation queue delivers to the human only the cases the model found hard, stripped of the easy cases that keep judgment calibrated, under time pressure, with the model's confidence displayed. The literature's answer is not to remove the human but to give the human an independent, practised role: blind review of a random, confidence-independent sample, judgment recorded before seeing the model's output, and metrics on human-model disagreement.

Learning from the engine's own outcomes adds two corruptions. Goodhart's law in Strathern's form, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", applies to any threshold tuned on a proxy ([Strathern 1997](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1062798700002660)). Ensign et al.'s runaway feedback loops in predictive policing arise because the model is trained on the crime reports its own deployments generate, and their fix generalises: feed back only data the model's own actions did not select ([Ensign et al. 2018](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/ensign18a.html)). Shumailov et al.'s model collapse warns against training on the engine's own decisions as labels at all ([Shumailov et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07566-y)). Accountability frameworks demand the complementary records: Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven's "meaningful human control" requires tracking (responsiveness to the humans' moral reasons, which for an engine are the explicit threshold policy and cost asymmetry) and tracing (a named human who understands the system and can be held responsible) ([Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2018.00015)); EU AI Act Article 14 requires that overseers of high-risk systems "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output", can "disregard, override or reverse the output", and can interrupt through a "stop" button ([Article 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)); and Nissenbaum's "problem of many hands" predicts responsibility diffuses to nobody unless logs identify the threshold-setter and model version as contributors ([Nissenbaum 1996](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02639315)).

### Verdict: substance in a narrow regime, pseudo-math outside it

There is intellectual substance here, and it is theorem-level. Six conditions, each with a literature behind it, jointly make the thesis correct: independence or decorrelation of errors (Condorcet, Ladha, Page, Satopää), so aggregation drives accuracy toward one; reversibility with bounded loss (Bezos, Popper, Taleb), so the loss distribution has no absorbing state and realised cost obeys the law of large numbers; fast, unambiguous outcome feedback (Kahneman-Klein, Hogarth, Tetlock, Ashby), so the loop closes and calibration improves, which is the true compounding term; parallel aggregation rather than serial chaining, so errors average instead of multiply; calibrated confidence with the option to abstain (Chow, Murphy), so the 80% average becomes 98% on the confidently handled 60% plus escalation on the rest, and "80%" was never the operative number; and small sizing relative to the whole (Kelly), so no single decision threatens the sequence. Under those conditions a cheap calibrated decider plus a threshold plus a feedback loop is a well-understood regulator and excellent outcomes are a consequence, not a hope.

The same thesis is wrong in the direction of catastrophe when decisions are chained (80% per step is 11% at ten), when errors are correlated through a shared model or prompt (effective crowd size near 1/rho, often near one, and extremizing a self-correlated aggregate manufactures false confidence), when any decision type is a one-way door (rare becomes certain at scale), when feedback is delayed or produced by the system's own actions (calibration drifts, Goodhart takes over, self-training collapses labels), when the human escalation path is nominal (the reviewer deskills, defers and becomes the crumple zone), and when "80% right" is quoted as a property of the model rather than of a (model, decision type, environment, period) tuple. The most compelling honest framing is three sentences. A calibrated cheap decider does not make 80%-good decisions; it sorts decisions into those it can make at 98% and those it should hand off, and the hand-off is the product. What compounds is not correctness but calibration: every observed outcome is reinvested in a better threshold, which is the only sense in which Hamming's compound interest applies. The system is safe exactly when its decisions are two-way doors, its errors are uncorrelated, its outcomes are observed and no single decision can end the sequence, and the engine's job is to enforce those properties, not to assume them.

## Every ingredient exists; nothing joins probability to threshold to outcome

### Rules, policy engines and flags give vocabulary but no probabilities

DMN gives the most mature vocabulary for composing decisions: decision requirements graphs with inputs, sub-decisions, knowledge sources and authority requirements; FEEL's three-valued booleans, which are precedent for "unknown" as a legitimate output; and the hit-policy catalogue (Unique, Any, Priority, First, Rule Order, Output Order, Collect with Sum/Max/Min/Count), which is a ready-made list of ways to combine several matching judgments into one answer ([OMG DMN](https://www.omg.org/spec/DMN/); [Drools DMN](https://docs.drools.org/latest/drools-docs/drools/DMN/index.html)). Camunda's rule that a UNIQUE violation "fails and an incident is raised" is exactly the behaviour a compounding engine needs when two calibrated judgments conflict above threshold: surface the conflict rather than pick silently ([Camunda hit policy](https://docs.camunda.io/docs/components/modeler/dmn/decision-table-hit-policy/)). But every DMN construct is deterministic; there is no slot for a probability, a confidence, a re-fittable threshold or an outcome feedback path, and Camunda 8's engine is not open for production use without a licence.

Policy engines separate decision from enforcement and return structured decisions with explanations. OPA's decision-log event carries `decision_id`, `input`, `result`, bundle `revision`, `timestamp`, `metrics`, masking pointers and `nd_builtin_cache` recording non-deterministic inputs "for debugging and decision replay"; it is the closest existing standard for a machine decision record ([OPA decision logs](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/latest/management-decision-logs/)). Cedar's algorithm, any `forbid` wins, else any `permit` allows, else default deny, with erroring policies skipped and reported in diagnostics, is the precedent for how a failed or timed-out judgment should be treated: never silently an approval, always carried in the record ([Cedar authorization](https://docs.cedarpolicy.com/auth/authorization.html)). Casbin makes the combination rule itself configuration (`e = some(where (p.eft == allow))`), the analogue of declaring and versioning the aggregation policy over probabilistic judgments ([Casbin](https://github.com/casbin/casbin)). None of the four represents uncertainty.

Feature-flag platforms already solve rollout of a decision rule. OpenFeature's evaluation result carries `value`, `variant`, `reason` and `errorCode` with the hard rule that "Flag evaluation calls must always return the default value in the event of abnormal execution", and its standard reasons (STATIC, DEFAULT, TARGETING_MATCH, SPLIT, CACHED, DISABLED, STALE, ERROR) are the start of a decision-provenance vocabulary ([OpenFeature flag evaluation](https://openfeature.dev/specification/sections/flag-evaluation)). LaunchDarkly's guarded rollouts ramp a variation while monitoring a guardrail metric with sequential testing, flag regression when the confidence interval lies entirely on the worse side of zero, and roll back automatically on regression or insufficient traffic, which is the rollout mechanism for a new threshold set or backend treated as "the variation" ([LaunchDarkly guarded rollouts](https://launchdarkly.com/docs/home/releases/guarded-rollouts)). What no flag platform has is a shadow mode for decisions or a standard join from decision to outcome; OpenFeature's Tracking API is still experimental ([OpenFeature tracking](https://openfeature.dev/specification/sections/tracking)).

### Bandits, routers and cascades learn thresholds offline, not from a ledger

Contextual bandits are the only mature prior art for learning policies from outcomes online. Microsoft's Decision Service formalised the loop as explore, log, learn, deploy, with logged propensities enabling unbiased off-policy evaluation ([arXiv 1606.03966](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03966)). Azure Personalizer added "apprentice mode", in which the service "returns the baseline action or default action that the application would have taken" while learning by imitation, plus a Rank/Reward split with delayed rewards joined by event ID; the service retires on 25 August 2026, leaving Vowpal Wabbit (BSD-3, with IPS, DM, DR and MTR estimators) and the Open Bandit Pipeline (Apache 2.0, with a dozen off-policy estimators) as the open stack ([Personalizer](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/personalizer/what-is-personalizer); [VW contextual bandits](https://github.com/VowpalWabbit/vowpal_wabbit/wiki/Contextual-Bandit-algorithms); [Open Bandit Pipeline](https://github.com/st-tech/zr-obp)). Apprentice mode is the template for shadow mode, and logging the propensity of the action taken is what lets a new threshold set be evaluated offline before rollout.

Routers and cascades are the prior art for learned cost-quality thresholds. RouteLLM thresholds a learned P(strong wins) against alpha, calibrates alpha to a target strong-call fraction, achieves 95% of GPT-4 quality with 14 to 26% strong-model calls, and was near random on MMLU until its preference-only training data was augmented with golden labels, a warning that thresholds must be fit on labelled outcomes for the task at hand ([arXiv 2406.18665](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665); [LMSYS blog](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)). ReDAct applied the same shape per decision inside agents, deferring about 15% of decisions to a large model to match its quality, with the threshold chosen so the calibration-set call rate hits a target ([arXiv 2604.07036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07036)). Neither router nor cascade logs decisions, tracks calibration drift or re-fits from production. Eval frameworks (DeepEval, Ragas, autoevals) give a stable `Score {name, score, metadata.rationale}` shape and a per-metric threshold, which is already a thresholded judge decision, but they are offline, hand-threshold the judge and never measure its calibration ([DeepEval](https://github.com/confident-ai/deepeval); [autoevals](https://github.com/braintrustdata/autoevals)). Guardrail frameworks standardise where a check runs (NeMo's input, dialog, retrieval, execution and output rails) and what happens on failure (Guardrails AI's `on_fail` menu of reask, fix, filter, refrain, noop, exception), and OpenAI's moderation endpoint returns per-category scores with the explicit advice to expect recalibration when the model updates, which is an argument that threshold fitting must be a repeatable versioned operation ([NeMo Guardrails](https://github.com/NVIDIA/NeMo-Guardrails); [Guardrails AI on_fail](https://guardrailsai.com/docs/hub/concepts/on_fail_policies); [OpenAI moderation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/moderation)). None has `escalate` or `shadow` as a first-class outcome.

### Agent harnesses standardised the interception points; only one published a confusion matrix

Every major harness now exposes the same four interception points, before a tool runs, after it returns, around a model call, and at stop or compaction, and most ship a human-approval primitive. Claude Code's `PreToolUse` hook returns `allow`, `deny`, `ask` or `defer` with a mandatory reason, the most complete gate vocabulary found, and permission rules evaluate "deny, then ask, then allow" with hooks unable to bypass deny rules ([Claude Code hooks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks); [permissions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permissions)). OpenAI's Agents SDK has input, output and tool guardrails with a `tripwire_triggered` verdict; LangChain has `wrap_tool_call` middleware, human-in-the-loop interrupts and deterministic retry and context-editing middleware; Vercel's eve has `auto()` approval policies that "ask a decision model whether to run the exact call or require user approval", defaulting to Jev and failing closed to a human on caution or failure; Cloudflare's `waitForApproval()` parks a run durably for months ([OpenAI Agents SDK guardrails](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/guardrails/); [LangChain middleware](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langchain/middleware/built-in); [eve](https://eve.dev/docs/human-in-the-loop); [Cloudflare Agents](https://developers.cloudflare.com/agents/guides/human-in-the-loop)). The Jev integrations differ on failure semantics instructively: eve `auto()` fails closed to a human (it authorises), LangChain's `AutoModeMiddleware` returns an error ToolMessage with no retry and a 30-message window that drops the user's request "around tool call 16", and the Hermes skill router fails open (it advises) ([LangChain TypeSafe docs](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/integrations/providers/typesafe.md); [Towards AI](https://towardsai.com/?p=53905); [Hermes docs](https://hermes-agent.nousresearch.com/docs/plugins/jev-skill-router)).

Only Anthropic has published a confusion matrix for a learned gate on real traffic, and it is the template. Claude Code's auto mode uses "a fast single-token yes/no filter tuned to err toward blocking" followed by chain-of-thought review only for flagged actions; on 10,000 real actions the **false-positive rate fell from 8.5% at stage one to 0.4% for the full pipeline**, while on 52 real overeager actions the miss rate rose from 6.6% to 17% and on 1,000 synthetic exfiltrations from 1.8% to 5.7%; deterministic tiers sit above and below it (in-project writes skip the classifier because they are checkpointed; critical-path removals are denied before the classifier sees them; the classifier never sees tool results, "to limit rationalization and prompt injection", with a separate injection probe); it fails closed, denying on no verdict; and users approve 93% of manual prompts, which is the latency the gate buys back ([Claude Code auto mode](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode); [permission-modes](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permission-modes)). Two 2026 results say the gate must be enforced by the environment, not requested of the model: in GateArena, with only a system-prompt instruction, two of three backbones attempted a withheld confirmation tool in 38% and 48% of episodes with no adversarial input, and a single-use confirmation token locked to parameter values cut irreversible harm from 37.5 to 60% down to 2.5% ([GateArena](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/168290)); and path-based authorisation reached full separation on matched pairs of identical irreversible actions differing only in justification, where keyword guardrails separated none ([ICML 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/77995)). Anthropic's own harness economics are the counterweight: a full planner-generator-evaluator harness was "more than 20x more expensive" than a solo run, and the evaluator is "unnecessary overhead" inside the model's solo-capability boundary, which makes "does this step need verification at all?" itself a cheap decision worth making ([Anthropic harness design](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)).

### Decision models converged on one wire format in sixteen days and all stop at the single question

Within sixteen days of Jev, OpenAI announced its Decisions API (`POST /v1/decisions` on `gpt-6-luna`, predicate/choice/score, $0.10 per million input, a different array-based schema with `choices[{value, description}]` and a `refusal` answer type), Amazon's Strands Labs released the open-source 1.9B Strands Decider that serves `POST /v1/systemone` locally, and Cloudflare released Clef (27B) and Clef-flash (9B) as Apache 2.0 open weights, "fully Jev-API compatible", with Clef-flash at **$0.038 per million input tokens, undercutting Jev's $0.042**, and a 38.8 ms competitor-measured median ([OpenAI Decisions](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions); [Strands Decider](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider); [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/clef-decision-models); [Cloudflare changelog](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-10-09-clef-omni-workers-ai/)). Microsoft Decision-1, Liquid d1, Perplexity's decider and a community Decision Index of roughly 70 open reproductions followed ([Jev Decision Index README](https://huggingface.co/spaces/multimodalart/jev-decision-index/raw/main/README.md)). The de-facto wire format is now `{state, questions: {id: {type, instructions, criteria}}}` to `{answers: {id: {probability | choice + probabilities + confidence | score + probabilities + confidence}}}`, and Vercel's AI Gateway already abstracts Jev, OpenAI, Microsoft, Liquid and Laya behind one `experimental_decide` call with "decision fallbacks" that rerun on another model when confidence or probability conditions match ([Vercel decision fallbacks](https://vercel.com/docs/ai-gateway/models-and-providers/decision-fallbacks)). Every vendor explicitly stops at the single question: TypeSafe says to decompose and "combine the answers with your own logic", OpenAI says dependent decisions need separate requests, Cloudflare evaluates questions "in isolation", and Strands' README reports that answers at confidence 0.9 or more "are right about 95% of the time", a calibration claim the engine should verify rather than trust ([How to build](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/how-to-build-with-system-one); [Strands Decider](https://github.com/strands-labs/strands-decider)). Confidence is also defined differently across the stack: Jev's is distribution concentration, DSPy's Noul confidence "measures distance from its threshold, not statistical calibration", and Vercel's prompted LLM fallbacks return `confidence: 0` and empty probabilities as sentinels ([DSPy 3.4.0](https://github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy/releases/tag/3.4.0); [Vercel decision fallbacks](https://vercel.com/docs/ai-gateway/models-and-providers/decision-fallbacks)).

The closest existing thing to "learning to compound" is DSPy 3.4.0, which makes `Noul` (with a `threshold`), `Score` (with `cuts`) and `Choice` (with `weights`) first-class output types whose parameters are "applied locally, not sent to the backend", decodes probability evidence from any generative LM or natively from TypeSafe, and ships ReAnchor, which fits those parameters against a program-level metric and accepts a setting only if it improves the training score and passes a held-out fold ([DSPy 3.4.0](https://github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy/releases/tag/3.4.0); [DSPy tutorial](https://dspy.ai/current/tutorials/jev_decisions/)). It is offline, single-program, budget-unbounded ("ReAnchor does not guarantee a fixed inference budget") and has no record of production decisions or outcomes. Durable-execution engines solve the resumability half of the chain problem: Temporal records every step in an Event History, "a complete, ordered record of everything that happened", replays deterministic workflow code against it, and pins in-flight executions to their original code via Worker Versioning, while warning that replay does not undo external effects, so "An agent that crashed after issuing a refund, but before recording that it did, may issue the refund again on restart" ([Understanding Temporal](https://docs.temporal.io/evaluate/understanding-temporal); [Temporal OpenAI Agents](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/python/integrations/openai-agents)). Nothing in them knows a step has a probability. Calibration tooling exists for classical models: NannyML's CBPE estimates classification performance without labels from calibrated probabilities, checks calibration on reference data and applies isotonic recalibration only if ECE improves; MAPIE and crepes provide conformal sets; TorchUncertainty provides ECE and temperature scaling ([NannyML](https://nannyml.readthedocs.io/en/stable/how_it_works/performance_estimation.html); [MAPIE](https://github.com/simai-ml/MAPIE)). None is wired to decision-model outputs or a decision ledger. Decision records exist in prose (Nygard's ADRs with supersession rather than editing; MADR's "Confirmation: how compliance with the decision will be checked") and in machine form (OPA logs), but no standard combines inputs, the probabilistic judgment, the policy version, the action taken and the eventual outcome ([Nygard](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions); [MADR](https://adr.github.io/madr/)).

### The gap in eight items

What does not exist, and what a compounding decision engine would provide, is a vendor-neutral decision record joining question and criteria version, state digest, backend and model version, raw probabilities, applied threshold and policy version, mode (live, shadow, escalated, overridden, failed-open), cost, latency and a later outcome event by id; composition semantics for probabilistic judgments that generalise DMN hit policies to log-odds sums, noisy-OR, minimum and conformal set size, with Camunda-style incidents when two confident judgments disagree; chain-level confidence accounting that carries running q^n per chain and decides where to insert cross-checks against a budget, FrugalGPT generalised from one query to a chain; shadow mode and guarded rollout for threshold sets and backends, with Temporal-style versioning so in-flight chains finish under the policy they started with; continuous threshold learning that runs ReAnchor-style cross-validated refits on ledger data with off-policy estimators over logged propensities; calibration monitoring per question and backend with CBPE-style label-free estimates and per-backend recalibration layers; abstention and escalation as first-class outcomes with durable human waits, in contrast to leaderboards and policy engines that score abstention as zero or deny; and a backend-agnostic judgment interface over the converged wire format with per-backend calibration records so backends can be swapped or cascaded. Apache 2.0, MIT or BSD components are available for every layer (OPA or Cedar, RouteLLM, DSPy, OBP, Vowpal Wabbit, MAPIE, NannyML, Evidently, NeMo Guardrails, Temporal); Camunda 8, Arize Phoenix (ELv2), the Inngest server (SSPL) and Unleash (AGPL) need licensing review before they sit in the core.

## The engine must enforce seven properties it cannot assume

### Measure independence and expose correlation as a parameter

Every pooling path must take a correlation parameter and the documentation must say to measure it, because the single most consequential finding across the math and the deployments is that redundancy without independence buys nothing. The procedure is mechanical: on a labelled set draw m repeated or variant calls per item, compute pairwise error correlation rho_bar and n_eff = n / (1 + (n - 1) rho_bar), or fit the beta-binomial rho from the distribution of correct counts, and compare observed majority-of-m accuracy to the independent-voter table; the gap is the correlation tax. Hosted Jev's near-zero run-to-run variance (std dev 0.0102) means self-consistency ensembles of the same question add essentially nothing, so the only decorrelators available are different framings, different decompositions, different option orders, different backends and different modalities of evidence, and the one study of wording found the best targeted question beat the best generic readout by only +0.006 AUROC out of sample ([arXiv 2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29429)). Combine sources by log-odds-weighted voting with weights from measured per-source accuracy, never by thresholding sub-answers and ANDing them, since that lost on 9 of 10 benchmarks. Treat the escalate-to-frontier-model path as a correlated vote until measured otherwise, since LLM fallbacks repeated Jev's confident errors more than 85% of the time, and prefer deterministic verification and blind human review as the independent signals ([arXiv 2609.29769](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29769)).

### Declare reversibility and never automate a one-way door

Reversibility and blast radius must be required fields of every decision type, because they convert a per-step accuracy requirement into a per-irreversible-step requirement and because ruin that is possible per decision is certain at scale. Mutating actions carry nearly all compounding risk (SABER's 92 to 96% odds effect), models will not reliably pause on their own (38 to 48% confirmation skips), and the fix that worked was environment-enforced (2.5% harm with locked tokens) ([arXiv 2512.07850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07850); [GateArena](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/168290)). Claude Code's tiering is an implicit two-way-door classifier: checkpointed in-project writes skip the gate, externally visible actions are gated, catastrophic ones are hard-denied ([permission-modes](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permission-modes)). The engine should classify by reversibility first, then set the threshold per class (Vercel's guidance is about 0.7 for read-only and 0.9 or higher for destructive with confirmation below), concentrate verification budget on mutating steps, and return at most `review` for any decision type declared a one-way door regardless of confidence, which is the Taleb-Kelly constraint made structural rather than probabilistic ([Vercel KB](https://vercel.com/kb/guide/typesafe-jev-and-ai-sdk)). Cap per-decision and per-window exposure the way fractional Kelly caps a stake.

### Floors only raise caution; fail-open means review

The combination of a hard rule and a soft judgment must be monotone: deterministic floors (deny lists, allowlists, critical-path checks, required flags) set a minimum caution level that the model can raise but never lower. This is the production architecture every measured gate uses: fail-closed floors, a learned judgment in the middle, and escalation for the uncertain tail, with the learned layer's failure mode determined by whether it authorises (fail closed) or advises (fail open) ([Claude Code auto mode](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode); [eve](https://eve.dev/docs/human-in-the-loop)). Steve Kinney's independent course states the rule as "Jev's judgments should fail open, so a timeout or low confidence changes nothing. Hard safety checks, like regular expressions and allowlists, should fail closed" and "the hard check sets a floor on the risk level, and Jev can raise risk above it but never lower it"; this ordering and the floor construction are his synthesis, not TypeSafe's, whose own guidance is to keep "control flow, deterministic rules, and side effects in code" and to compose answers in code ([Steve Kinney, Jev in practice](https://stevekinney.com/courses/ai-development-setup/jev-in-practice); [How to build](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/how-to-build-with-system-one)). "Fail open" needs a precise meaning: an oracle error, timeout, malformed response or confidence-unavailable sentinel yields a neutral verdict flagged as failed-open whose default action is review, never act, mirroring Cedar's skip-and-report, OpenFeature's return-the-default, and Vercel's rule that `confidenceBelow` matches conservatively when no finite confidence exists ([Cedar authorization](https://docs.cedarpolicy.com/auth/authorization.html); [OpenFeature flag evaluation](https://openfeature.dev/specification/sections/flag-evaluation); [Vercel decision fallbacks](https://vercel.com/docs/ai-gateway/models-and-providers/decision-fallbacks)). The gate must be enforced by the environment at a hook, not requested of the model, and it is not a security boundary: a single-gate block was bypassed by writing a file through a bash heredoc, and blocks are "an instruction, not a control" ([ten-levels-of-jev](https://github.com/disler/ten-levels-of-jev)).

### Verify at checkpoints, ratchet, and retry from a clean context

Sequential work must be decomposed into single checkable decisions with the strictest available verifier after each, deterministic first (tests, schema, re-execution, DB state) and model-based second, with resampling on rejection from the last verified state and the failed attempt removed from context, optionally with a paraphrased prompt or different backend to decorrelate. Track per-step q and compute q^n per chain as workflow state; the required per-step q for n steps at target T is T^(1/n), so 100 steps at 95% needs q of 0.99949, which an 80% oracle reaches only with a 99%-catch verifier or lead-by-8 voting. Ratchet verified state so that cost is linear, and document that ratcheting fixes cost, not correctness. Red-flag and discard malformed or overlong outputs before they are counted, as MAKER did, and never let a bare label pass from one step to the next without the probability and cues that produced it, so downstream steps cannot cascade on a label ([MAKER](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09030v1)). Anthropic's finding that unit tests and curl "were not enough" and that end-to-end browser testing was required for the model to notice broken features is the reminder that the verifier must observe ground truth, not the generator's description of it ([Anthropic long-running harnesses](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)).

### Shadow by default; learn only from rows the engine did not select

New engines, thresholds and backends must change nothing until flipped, with the apprentice-mode semantics of returning the incumbent decision while recording and scoring the candidate's; the one published week-long shadow run led to removal, which is the argument for making it mandatory rather than optional ([dev.to](https://dev.to/shimo4228/is-there-any-point-to-this-removing-the-jev-plugins-i-added-to-claude-code-after-one-week-49eh)). Promotion from shadow to live should run as a guarded rollout with a sequential test on a guardrail metric, minimum sample per step and automatic rollback, and in-flight chains should finish under the policy they started with. Learning must read only the ledger and fit thresholds only on rows whose observation did not depend on the engine's own confidence-gated choice: shadow rows, blind audits and a randomised exploration slice where a different mechanism decided. A fit on confidence-selected rows is biased by construction (Ensign's runaway loop), must be labelled as such and treated as a fallback, and the engine should never train on its own decisions as labels (Shumailov's collapse) ([Ensign et al. 2018](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/ensign18a.html); [Shumailov et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07566-y)). Fit per (question, criteria version, backend version, action class), not globally, because calibration is target-specific: Vals saw it fail to transfer between two tasks, an early-outcome predictor showed persistent per-agent calibration gaps of 0.11 to 0.14, and the per-benchmark F1-optimal threshold was 0.35 ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/independent-evaluation-of-jev); [arXiv 2609.25647](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25647); [arXiv 2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29429)). Use ReAnchor's acceptance rule, improve the training score and pass a held-out fold, and log propensities so off-policy estimators can evaluate a candidate threshold set before it is promoted. Keep the human reviewer calibrated rather than merely available: route a random, confidence-independent sample to blind judgment recorded before the model's output is shown, and report human-model disagreement as a first-class metric.

### Abstain as a first-class result and record every decision as a bet

The decision API should return a four-valued outcome, act, review, refuse and defer, each with a mandatory reason, and should treat the review band as defined by margin rather than a single cutoff, since decisions within about 0.1 of the threshold are the ones that flip under order changes and adversarial evidence ([arXiv 2609.28613](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.28613)). Thresholds are statements of values and should be stored as the cost asymmetry that justified them (act iff q > C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN)), reviewed like any other decision-quality element and owned by a named person per decision type, because tracking and tracing require it and because many-hands diffusion otherwise lands blame on the nearest human ([Elkan 2001](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~elkan/rescale.pdf); [Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2018.00015)). Every decision is a bet and the record should contain the pre-outcome probability, the threshold version, the mode and an outcome deadline, so Brier reliability and resolution can be computed separately per type and the engine's headline metric can be per-type calibration and abstention quality over time rather than aggregate hit rate. Monitor with CBPE-style label-free estimates between outcome arrivals, require about 250 to 400 outcomes per confidence bin before trusting a bin, and use conformal set size as the principled abstention test for Choice questions: a set with more than one member at target coverage should not be auto-acted on.

### State hygiene, option rotation and backend discipline

The adapter layer must do in code what the jaggedness list says the model cannot: arithmetic, counting, date comparison, unit and colour conversion, and pruning of irrelevant state, since pre-digestion moved one task from 74.3% to 100% and TypeSafe's own workaround for counting is one Noul per item summed in code ([jev-state](https://github.com/suranjaychandra/jev-state); [Jev 1.13 jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13)). Treat state as data and keep untrusted content out of it: Check Point showed typed input and "untrusted" markers do nothing, Claude Code strips tool results from its gate and runs a separate injection probe, and any decision that depends on untrusted text should route to a slower stage or a deterministic check ([Check Point](https://blog.checkpoint.com/ai-security/jev-is-not-a-language-model-but-it-breaks-like-one-prompt-injection-against-a-typed-decision-model/); [Claude Code auto mode](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode)). Rotate or double-check Choice option order when a decision sits near the boundary, phrase Nouls so that high means yes, add an `other` or `none of the above` option because Jev "always picks an answer", and never read `confidence` as calibration. Pin versioned model IDs, log the response `model` field on every decision, and treat an alias move (announced for about 21 October) as a threshold-invalidating event; batch every question that shares a state into one request and account cost by state size; isolate each vendor behind a versioned adapter that tolerates extra envelope fields and different probability key types, since TypeSafe's Score keys are strings, OpenAI's are integers inside objects, and gateways add `id`, `provider` and `usage.cost` ([Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models); [OpenAI Decisions](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions)). Keep the engine's own value in its policy, ledger and learning so it is a "Customer Application" rather than a prohibited pass-through, and get legal review before any downstream classifier is trained on Jev outputs ([MCA](https://typesafe.ai/legal/mca)). Finally, make "does this step need a judgment at all?" a cheap decision, since routing results consistently send 70 to 85% of calls down the cheap path at 95% quality and an evaluator inside the model's solo-capability boundary is overhead ([LMSYS blog](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/); [Anthropic harness design](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes the product. A compounding decision engine is not a machine that makes 80%-good decisions faster; it is a sorting-and-learning loop whose raw material is a cheap, repeatable, well-ranked but task-uncalibrated probability stream, and whose output is a partition of decisions into those a code-owned policy will act on at a measured error rate, those it will hand to an independent verifier or a practised human, and those it will refuse. Jev is a credible first backend for that loop, with a stable wire format now shared by four vendors and open weights undercutting its price within a month, but every number that matters to the loop, the per-task threshold, the correlation between its votes and any fallback's, the reversibility of each action, the ECE in each bin, is something the engine must measure on its own ledger, because TypeSafe publishes none of them and the independent measurements span an order of magnitude.

Two consequences are not obvious from the thesis as stated. First, the pivotal engineering quantity is not accuracy but the correlation ceiling: whether to buy another cheap vote, escalate, or verify deterministically is decided by comparing the required accuracy against 1 - rho(1 - p), and the measured 85% overlap between Jev's confident errors and LLM fallbacks says the standard "escalate to a bigger model" branch is often a second copy of the same vote. Second, the ratchet metaphor is exactly half right: locking in verified progress turns exponential retry cost into linear cost, but it also locks in every accepted-wrong step at rate 1 - q, so the ratchet's trustworthiness is entirely the verifier's catch rate, and the honest claim for the engine is "linear cost at a measured latent-error rate", not "reliable". Built around enforcing those two facts, plus declared reversibility, monotone floors, shadow-by-default, unselected-row learning and first-class abstention, the engine turns the apparent weakness ("only 80%") into the mechanism ("calibrated enough to know which 20% to abstain on"), and that is the version of the thesis that survives every literature surveyed here.
